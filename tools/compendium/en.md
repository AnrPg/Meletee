# Study Techniques & Advice Compendium

Oct 8, 2026 · @ANR

## Start here: how to use this compendium and find your own method

This compendium collects the study techniques and habits that learning research supports best, explained in plain words with examples from many fields. It is written for any student: at school, at university, in a training programme or teaching yourself, whether you study languages, law, history, maths, physics, programming, music, economics or medicine. You do not need to read it from start to finish. Skim "How learning works" first (it explains why the techniques work), glance at the time-cost table to see what each technique costs, then pick two or three techniques to try. The section below shows you how to test them on yourself so you end up with a method that fits you, not someone else.

### Finding your method: how to experiment and track what works

Treat finding a study method like a small experiment: try one change at a time, measure it honestly, and keep what works. The aim is a method that suits you and that you enjoy enough to keep using.

Most students choose methods by feel, and feel is a poor guide. In one survey of university students, about 84% listed re-reading as a study strategy and only about 11% listed testing themselves, even though self-testing works much better (Karpicke, Butler & Roediger 2009). The steps below replace "it felt productive" with evidence you collect yourself.

### Step 1: Start from a strong default

Begin with retrieval and spacing (blank-page recall, flashcards, questions). Adjust from there rather than starting from scratch.

**Why it works.** Decades of research agree that pulling information out of memory, at growing intervals, is the most reliable base for almost any subject. Starting there is like a new cook starting from a tested recipe: you can change the spices later, but you are not inventing bread from nothing.

**How to do it.** For each study block, read or watch once, then close the source and recall (write, sketch or say it). Turn the key facts into a few flashcards or questions. Review them on later days.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A secondary-school student learning French vocabulary reads the list once, covers the French column, and tries to write each word from the English. Missed words go on cards for tomorrow.
- *Non-trivial:* A law student starts with the default for contract law (recall the elements of each doctrine, then cards for case names), and only later adds case-comparison tables once she sees that confusing similar cases is her main error.

### Step 2: Change one thing at a time

For example, try concept maps instead of flashcards for one subject for one or two weeks.

**Why it works.** If you change three things at once (new app, new time of day, new method) and your results improve, you will not know which change helped. Scientists hold everything else fixed so the one thing they vary can show its effect, and the same logic works for you.

**How to do it.** Write down the one change you are testing ("concept maps instead of cards for biochemistry"). Keep your time of day, place and total minutes roughly the same as before.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A history student keeps everything the same but swaps "re-read the chapter" for "blank-page timeline from memory" for two weeks.
- *Non-trivial:* A programmer learning a new framework tests "build a tiny project after each tutorial" against "follow the tutorial and take notes". He uses the same tutorial series for both so the content is not the hidden difference, and alternates the methods across similar lessons.

### Step 3: Give it enough time

At least 5 sessions over 1 to 2 weeks. Every new method feels awkward at first.

**Why it works.** The first few sessions measure how unfamiliar the method is, not how good it is. Think of switching to a new keyboard layout: you type slower for days before you type faster. Effortful methods like recall also feel worse than re-reading even when they work better, so a one-day trial is biased against them.

**How to do it.** Put the trial dates in your calendar. Decide in advance that you will not judge the method before session 5.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student trying Anki for a geography course feels slow on day 1 and day 2, but by day 6 reviews take half the time and recall is clearly better.
- *Non-trivial:* A music student trying to learn a piece by memorising it in small sections from the end backwards finds the first week frustrating. By the second week she notices the endings she used to stumble on are now the most secure part, a benefit she would never have seen in a two-day trial.

### Step 4: Rate every session

How much did I recall the next day (1 to 5)? How much did I enjoy it? How focused was I? How much effort did it take?

**Why it works.** Memory of how a week went is vague and biased by the last session. A quick rating after each session builds a small data set. The next-day recall question matters most, because it measures learning after some forgetting, not the warm glow straight after study.

**How to do it.** Keep a note or a spreadsheet with one row per session. Rate enjoyment, focus and effort at the end of the session; rate recall at the start of the next day, after a quick 2-minute test on yesterday's material.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A biology student ends each session with three numbers in her phone's notes app: enjoy 4, focus 3, effort 4. Next morning she adds recall 3.
- *Non-trivial:* An economics student notices that his "effort" score is high on days his recall is also high, and low on days recall is poor. That pattern tells him that sessions that feel easy are the ones to be suspicious of.

### Step 5: Use objective signals too

Question-bank scores, how many gaps appear in blank-page recall, and how well topics hold up at the 7-day review.

**Why it works.** Ratings are still feelings. Objective signals are harder to fool yourself with: a score or a count of gaps does not care how fluent the page felt. Students are generally overconfident after re-reading and better calibrated after testing, so the objective signal is your reality check.

**How to do it.** Pick one or two numbers you can collect without extra effort: percentage correct on practice questions, number of items missed on a blank-page recall, or number of flashcards you failed at the 7-day review.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A maths student counts how many of 10 practice problems he solves without looking at the worked solution.
- *Non-trivial:* A medical student tracks question-bank scores per topic and sees that cardiology is at 75% but drops to 55% when the questions are mixed with respiratory topics. The method she used produced knowledge that only works when she already knows which chapter a question comes from, so she adds interleaving.

### Step 6: Decide: keep, tweak or drop

Keep what scores well on both recall and enjoyment. Tweak what has good recall but feels boring. Drop what you enjoy but can't recall.

**Why it works.** A method has to pass two tests: it must work (recall) and you must actually keep doing it (enjoyment). A brilliant method you abandon after a week is worth less than a good method you use every day. This rule makes the decision explicit instead of drifting.

**How to do it.**

1. Average the recall and enjoyment ratings for the trial.
2. High recall and high enjoyment: keep.
3. High recall, low enjoyment: tweak the format (shorter sessions, a study partner, a different app, standing up).
4. Low recall, high enjoyment: drop it as a main method; keep it as a warm-up if you like it.
5. Low on both: drop.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A language learner loves watching films with subtitles but recalls few new words the next day. He keeps films for Friday evenings and does cards on weekdays.
- *Non-trivial:* A physics student finds blank-page recall very effective but dull. Instead of dropping it, she tweaks it: she explains the derivation aloud while walking, and records herself. Recall stays high and enjoyment rises from 2 to 4.

### Step 7: Match methods to content

One person may end up with flashcards for vocabulary, drawing for processes and worked problems for maths.

**Why it works.** Different material has different structure. Arbitrary facts (dates, vocabulary, drug names) need repetition; processes need a picture of the sequence; skills need practice. One method rarely fits everything, the way one tool does not fit every screw.

**How to do it.** Sort each course's content into rough types: facts, processes, concepts, skills. Run your trials per type, and write down the winner for each.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A chemistry student uses cards for the names of functional groups and practice problems for reaction mechanisms.
- *Non-trivial:* A medicine student ends up with flashcards for pharmacology, drawing for physiology and cases for pathology. A law student in the same building uses cards for case names, flowcharts for procedure, and essay outlines for policy debates. Both arrived there by testing, not by copying each other.

### Step 8: Review monthly

Methods that worked for one course or year may need to change for the next.

**Why it works.** Your material, your workload and your skill as a learner all change. A method that suited first-year anatomy (lots of names) may not suit clinical years (lots of reasoning), and what suited beginner Spanish (vocabulary) may not suit advanced Spanish (fluency and nuance).

**How to do it.** Once a month, spend 15 minutes with your session log. Ask: what is my weakest subject or content type now, and which method am I using there? Pick one new experiment for the coming month.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student moving from introductory to intermediate statistics notices his card-heavy method no longer helps with choosing the right test, and adds mixed practice problems.
- *Non-trivial:* An adult learning to code notices that tutorials worked well for the first three months but her progress has stalled. Her log shows high enjoyment but falling recall of how to structure programs on her own. She switches to building small projects from a blank file, which feels slower but shows steady gains.

### Things worth tracking

Each signal below answers a different question. Together they tell you whether a method works, whether you will keep doing it, and where to spend your next hour.

- **Study sessions: date, course, method, minutes, recall rating, enjoyment, focus.** This is the core of the experiment. Minutes let you compare methods per unit of time, not just per session. Enjoyment and focus predict whether you will keep going. The recall rating should be taken the next day: same-day recall mostly measures what is still in short-term memory, and a feeling of fluency right after study is a poor predictor of what you will know in a week.
- **Topics: when learned, review dates, how each review went.** This shows the shape of your own forgetting. If a topic is solid at day 3 but shaky at day 14, your gaps between reviews are too long for that kind of material. If reviews are always easy, you can stretch the gaps and save time.
- **Question scores per topic over time.** The most objective measure you have, and the closest to an exam. A rising line means the method is working for that topic. A flat line despite many hours means time is going in but not sticking. Compare scores on blocked sets (one topic) with mixed sets: a big drop on mixed sets means you know the content but cannot yet tell when to use it.
- **Weekly reflection: what worked, what didn't, one change for next week.** Numbers tell you what happened; reflection tells you why. "Recall was low on Wednesday" becomes useful once you note "I studied after a 9-hour shift". Limiting yourself to one change keeps the experiment clean (step 2).
- **Wins and small milestones.** These are not vanity. Seeing progress is one of the strongest drivers of motivation, and a list of wins is the evidence you need on a bad day that the method does work.

### A worked example: one 2-week experiment

*This is an illustration with made-up but realistic numbers, not data from a study.*

Maya is a second-year psychology student. She usually re-reads her notes and highlights them. She wants to know whether blank-page recall works better for her. She picks two similar topics from the same module, each with about the same number of pages and the same lecturer: "Memory" and "Attention".

**Setup.**

- Topic A (Memory): her usual method, re-read and highlight, 40 minutes per session.
- Topic B (Attention): read once, then blank-page recall and fill gaps in a second colour, 40 minutes per session.
- Five sessions per topic over 2 weeks, alternating days so neither topic always gets the "fresh" morning slot.
- After each session: enjoyment, focus and effort (1 to 5). Next morning: a 2-minute quiz of 5 questions on yesterday's material, scored as recall (1 to 5).
- On day 14: a 20-question practice quiz on each topic, from the module's question bank.

**Results (averages over 5 sessions).**

| Signal | A: Re-read + highlight | B: Blank-page recall |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How well I felt I knew it (end of session) | 4.4 | 3.0 |
| Next-day recall (1 to 5) | 2.6 | 3.8 |
| Enjoyment | 3.4 | 3.0 |
| Focus | 2.8 | 4.0 |
| Effort | 2.0 | 4.0 |
| Day-14 quiz (20 questions) | 11/20 (55%) | 16/20 (80%) |

**What Maya learns.**

1. Her feeling of knowing was highest for the method that produced the least learning. That gap is the fluency illusion in her own data.
2. Blank-page recall was harder (effort 4.0) and slightly less enjoyable, but it held up the next day and at two weeks.
3. Focus was much higher with recall, because she could not drift while producing answers.

**Decision.** Keep blank-page recall as her main method. Because enjoyment was a little lower, tweak it: she will do the recall with a friend once a week and compare pages. She keeps a 5-minute skim of her notes as a warm-up, not as the main study. Next month's experiment: adding flashcards for the definitions she keeps missing.

**Limits.** Two topics are never perfectly equal, and one student is not a study. If Maya wanted more confidence she could swap the methods for the next two topics and see if the pattern repeats.

## How learning works: the principles behind every technique

Most lasting learning comes from two habits: pulling information out of memory (retrieval) and doing it again at growing intervals (spacing). Everything else in this document either supports those two or makes them easier to keep up.

The seven principles below explain why. Once you understand them, you can judge any study method yourself, including ones this compendium does not mention: ask whether it makes you produce answers from memory, whether it spreads that effort over time, and whether it builds meaning rather than just familiarity.

### Recognising is not remembering

Re-reading makes material feel familiar, and that feeling of fluency is easily mistaken for knowing it. The only real test is whether you can produce it with the book closed.

**Why it works.** Recognition and recall are different jobs. Recognising a face in a crowd is easy; drawing that face from memory is hard. When you re-read, the page supplies the answer and your brain only has to say "yes, I've seen this", which feels like knowing. In an exam, a conversation or a real task, nothing supplies the answer, and you need recall. The danger is that fluency (how easily something reads) is what your brain uses to judge how well you know it, and fluency comes cheaply from repetition.

**How to do it.**

1. After reading a section, close the source.
2. Write or say the main points, or answer one question about it.
3. Open the source and check. Only what you produced counts as known.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* You read a recipe twice and it seems obvious. In the kitchen without the book, you cannot remember whether the butter goes in before or after the flour.
- *Non-trivial:* A programmer reads a tutorial on recursion and every line makes sense. Asked to write a function that reverses a list recursively on a blank screen, she freezes. Following someone else's reasoning (recognition) is a different skill from building it yourself (recall), and only the second one transfers to new problems.

**Watch out.** Multiple-choice self-tests partly measure recognition. Use free recall or short answers to check yourself honestly.

### Forgetting is part of the process

Memory fades fast after first exposure (the forgetting curve). Each successful recall, especially when it takes some effort, makes the memory last longer.

**Why it works.** Picture a new memory as a leaky bucket. Right after you fill it, it leaks quickly; within a day much of it is gone. Each time you top it up by recalling, the bucket gets sturdier and leaks more slowly, so the next refill can wait longer. That is why review intervals can grow: 1 day, then 3, then a week, then a month. Retrieval also works like walking a path through a forest: the first walk is hard and the path barely shows, but each walk tramples it flatter, and a well-used path is easy to find even after weeks. Forgetting is not a sign you failed; a bit of forgetting before each review is what makes the next recall strengthen the path.

**How to do it.**

1. Expect to lose much of a new topic within a day or two. Plan the first review within 24 hours.
2. Space later reviews further apart each time the recall succeeds.
3. When recall fails, shorten the gap again.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* You learn 20 Spanish words on Monday. By Tuesday you recall 9. After reviewing Tuesday, Thursday and the next Monday, you recall 18 a month later with only a few minutes of total work.
- *Non-trivial:* A replication of Ebbinghaus's original experiment found the same steep early drop, and also a small bump in retention at 24 hours compared with shorter delays, plausibly because a night's sleep helped. The practical lesson: the first day matters most, and a review the next day catches material at the point where the bucket has leaked a lot but not emptied.

**Watch out.** Material you never understood does not "fade"; it was never stored well. Spacing helps keep what you learned, not fix what you missed.

### Desirable difficulties

Methods that feel harder in the moment (self-testing, spacing, mixing topics) produce better long-term memory than methods that feel smooth. A struggle to recall is a sign the method is working.

**Why it works.** Robert and Elizabeth Bjork distinguish two kinds of strength: how easily you can get at something right now (retrieval strength) and how deeply it is stored (storage strength). Easy practice boosts the first and barely touches the second. Effortful retrieval, when the memory is a bit faded, builds the second. It is like strength training: lifting a weight you can barely move builds muscle; lifting a feather a thousand times does not. The trap is that easy practice also feels better, so learners rate it as more effective.

**How to do it.**

1. Test yourself instead of re-reading.
2. Leave gaps between study sessions on the same topic.
3. Mix related topics or problem types in one session.
4. Try to generate an answer before you look it up.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A tennis player who practises forehand, backhand and volley in a random mix feels clumsier in practice than one who hits 50 forehands in a row, but plays better in a match, where shots come in random order.
- *Non-trivial:* In one study, people learned painters' styles either by seeing each painter's works grouped together or mixed with other painters. Mixed study led to better recognition of new paintings, yet most participants believed grouped study had helped them more, even after the test. A maths student faces the same trap: doing ten problems of one type in a row feels smooth, but mixing types forces you to practise choosing the method, which is what the exam asks.

**Watch out.** A difficulty is only desirable if you can overcome it with effort. If you can recall nothing at all, the gap was too long or the material was never understood. Go back, re-learn, then test again.

### Understanding before memorising

Facts tied to a mechanism ("why") are easier to keep and to use than isolated facts. Most subjects are cumulative: what you understand now carries into what you learn next.

**Why it works.** Memory is a web, not a list. A new fact that links to things you already know has many threads to hang from, so there are many routes back to it. An isolated fact hangs from one thread and drops easily. Understanding also lets you rebuild a forgotten detail: if you know why something happens, you can often work out the detail again. And because knowledge builds on itself (algebra into calculus, grammar into fluent writing, anatomy and physiology into pathology, pharmacology and clinical years in medicine), a weak foundation makes every later topic harder.

**How to do it.**

1. Before memorising details, make sure you can explain the main idea in two or three sentences.
2. For each new fact, ask "why is this true?" and "how does it connect to what I know?".
3. Only then put the details on cards.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Memorising that "the Romans built roads straight" is easy to forget. Knowing they were built to move armies quickly and to be easy to survey makes the fact stick and helps you guess other features of Roman roads.
- *Non-trivial:* An economics student memorises "when interest rates rise, bond prices fall" and keeps mixing up the direction. Once he understands why (an old bond paying 2% is worth less when new bonds pay 4%, so its price must drop until its yield matches), he never confuses it again and can also reason about related cases he was never taught.

**Watch out.** Some material has no logic to understand (vocabulary, names, dates). For that, use memory techniques and cards directly, without waiting for a "why" that does not exist.

### Generation beats consumption

Anything you produce yourself, such as an answer, a diagram, an explanation or a question, is remembered better than something you only read or watched.

**Why it works.** When you generate something, your brain has to search memory, choose, and build. That work leaves a stronger, more connected trace than passively receiving the finished product. It is the difference between being driven somewhere and driving there yourself: the passenger sees the same streets but the driver learns the route. A meta-analysis of the "generation effect" found a moderate, reliable advantage for generating over reading across many kinds of material.

**How to do it.**

1. Before reading the answer, try to produce it, even as a guess.
2. Turn notes into questions and answer them.
3. Draw the diagram from memory instead of copying it.
4. Explain the idea in your own words, not the textbook's.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Completing "The capital of Australia is C\_\_\_\_\_" (Canberra) is remembered better than reading "The capital of Australia is Canberra".
- *Non-trivial:* A student learning to play jazz piano transcribes a short solo by ear instead of reading a printed transcription. It takes ten times longer, but she remembers the phrases and can reuse them in her own playing. A history student who writes his own exam question on the causes of the First World War, then answers it, learns more than one who reads a model answer, because choosing what to ask already forces him to decide what matters.

**Watch out.** Generating wrong answers is fine as long as you check them soon and correct them. Generation without feedback can lock in errors.

### Sleep consolidates memory

What you study is stabilised during sleep, so an all-nighter costs the very memories it was meant to build.

**Why it works.** During the day, new memories are held in a fragile, temporary form. During sleep, especially deep slow-wave sleep, the brain replays them and moves them into more lasting storage, like saving a draft document so it survives a restart. Sleep before learning matters too: after a night without sleep, the brain is worse at taking in new memories at all. So an all-nighter hurts twice: it weakens what you studied the day before and makes the next day's studying less effective.

**How to do it.**

1. Aim for 7 to 9 hours, at roughly regular times.
2. Study the hardest new material earlier in the evening rather than late at night, then sleep.
3. Before an exam, protect the night's sleep over one more late session.
4. A short nap after learning can help when the night was short.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A child practising a new piano piece often plays it better the morning after than at the end of the evening's practice, without any extra practice in between.
- *Non-trivial:* A student has a statistics exam at 9 am and three topics left. Staying up until 4 am to cover all three leaves her tired, slower at reasoning and with weaker memory of the earlier topics. Covering two topics well, sleeping 7 hours, and skimming the third over breakfast is the better bet, because sleep protects what she has already learned.

**Watch out.** Caffeine late in the day and screens in bed shorten deep sleep even when you sleep the same number of hours.

### Cramming vs deep learning

Cramming works for an exam the next morning and fails a month later. Spaced retrieval takes about the same total time but is spread out, and it keeps working long after the exam.

**Why it works.** Cramming fills the leaky bucket to the brim right before the test, and for a few hours it holds. Then it leaks as fast as ever, because nothing made it sturdier. Spacing uses the same total time to refill the bucket several times, and each refill makes it leak more slowly. The cruel part is that cramming feels efficient: performance during a cram session is high, so it seems to work. Research on review gaps suggests a useful rule: the longer you need to remember something, the longer your gaps between reviews should be.

**How to do it.**

1. Split your total study time for a topic into several shorter sessions on different days.
2. Start each session with recall of the earlier material before adding new.
3. For an exam in a week, review every day or two; for an exam in a few months, space reviews a week or more apart.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Two students each spend 3 hours on a list of chemistry formulas. One spends 3 hours the night before; the other spends 30 minutes on six different days. Both pass the quiz. Only the second still knows the formulas in next term's course that assumes them.
- *Non-trivial:* A law student needs case law both for the end-of-term exam and for the bar exam two years later. Cramming gets her through the first, but she will have to re-learn everything for the second. Spacing over the term costs no extra time now and leaves knowledge that only needs light review later. Knowledge you will build on (core maths, a language's grammar, basic sciences in medicine) is exactly what you should never cram.

**Watch out.** If an exam is tomorrow and you have not started, cramming is better than nothing. Use active recall while you cram, and sleep for at least a few hours.

## Time cost at a glance

A "paragraph" means one dense paragraph of about 150 words that you have already read once. Every other technique is expressed as a multiple of that 30-second question.

### Retention per minute vs depth

Cheap techniques (recall questions, flashcards, practice questions) give the most retention per minute. They should be the daily base of your studying, the way staple foods are the base of a diet. Expensive techniques (teaching, concept maps, summaries, Feynman write-ups) give less retention per minute, but they buy something the cheap ones do not: a connected picture of the topic, the ability to explain, and the ability to handle questions you have never seen. That depth is worth paying for, but only for the 10-20% of ideas that everything else hangs on. Spending 40 minutes making a concept map of a list of vocabulary is waste; spending it on the central mechanism of a course is often the best hour of the week.

### Why teaching costs about 50× a recall question and is still worth it

Teaching a paragraph to a friend, including preparing, costs roughly 40-80 recall questions' worth of time. For an ordinary detail that is a poor trade: 50 well-spaced recall questions would fix it in memory many times over. But teaching does three things recall does not. Preparing forces you to organise the idea from the start, find the order in which it makes sense, and anticipate questions. Explaining exposes the exact spot where your understanding stops ("and then it... somehow works"). And the listener's questions push you to connect the idea to things you would not have linked yourself. So choose carefully: teach the key concept of a topic (supply and demand, natural selection, recursion, the cardiac cycle), and use recall and cards for the details that hang from it.

### A sample budget: one hour on a 10-paragraph chapter

*This is an illustration based on the estimates above, not a measured result.*

**A passive hour.** Re-read the chapter twice (about 20 minutes), then highlight it (about 15 minutes), then re-read the highlights (about 10 minutes), with the rest spent rewriting a few points neatly. The chapter feels very familiar by the end. Almost all of that time is low-value: you have practised recognising the text, not recalling it, and you have no idea which parts you would fail on a test.

**An active hour.**

| Step | Time | What it buys |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pretest: 5 questions before reading | 5 min | Attention primed for the answers |
| Read the chapter once, carefully | 15 min | First exposure |
| Blank-page recall of the whole chapter, then fill gaps from the book in another colour | 15 min | Your gaps, found and fixed |
| Make 8-10 flashcards for the facts you missed | 10 min | A month of cheap spaced review |
| 3 exam-style practice questions, with explanations | 7 min | Application and a reality check |
| Feynman write-up of the one key idea | 8 min | Deep understanding where it counts |
| **Total** | **60 min** |  |

The active hour reads the chapter only once, yet it produces about 20 retrieval attempts, a list of known gaps, a set of cards for future reviews, and one idea you can explain in plain words. It also feels harder and less satisfying than the passive hour, which is exactly the trap described in "How learning works": the hour that feels best is not the hour that teaches most.

### Active recall (practice testing)

Close the source and answer questions, write what you remember, or explain it aloud. Then check. It works for every subject and should be the core of every study session.

**Why it works.** A memory is less like a file in a cabinet and more like a path through a field: every time you walk it, it gets easier to find. Re-reading shows you the path from above; recall makes you walk it. Pulling something out of memory changes the memory itself, strengthening it and adding new routes to it. It also gives you honest feedback. Re-reading makes everything feel familiar, but only a closed-book attempt tells you whether you can produce the answer when it counts.

**How to do it.**

1. Read or listen to a section once, with full attention.
2. Close the book or hide the notes.
3. Answer questions about it (your own, the textbook's, or the learning objectives turned into questions), out loud or on paper.
4. Check against the source and mark what you missed or got wrong.
5. Come back to the misses later, not straight away (see spaced repetition).

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* After a Spanish lesson, cover the English column of your vocabulary list and say each Spanish word's meaning before checking.
- *Non-trivial:* A history student reads about the causes of the First World War, then asks herself "What would have happened if Britain had stayed neutral?" She cannot answer from the text directly, so she has to retrieve several facts and combine them. Recall that forces you to use knowledge, not just repeat it, is the strongest version, and it also shows which links between facts are missing.

**⏱ Time cost.** About 30 seconds per question (1×, the baseline). Three questions on a paragraph take about the same time as re-reading it twice, and leave far more behind.

**Watch out.**

- Checking too early. Peeking after two seconds turns recall back into re-reading. Give each question a real attempt first.
- Asking only "what" questions. Mix in "why", "how" and "what if" so you test understanding, not just labels.
- Mistaking difficulty for failure. Struggling to recall is the workout, not a sign that the method isn't working.

### Spaced repetition

Review the same material at growing intervals (for example 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days). Apps like Anki schedule it for you. It suits high-volume facts and anything you need months later.

**Why it works.** Each time you recall something, its forgetting slows down, so the next review can wait longer. The key is timing. Retrieving a memory that is half-forgotten is the workout: the effort of finding it is what makes it stronger. Retrieving one you saw ten minutes ago is a stroll, pleasant but with little training effect. Spacing deliberately lets a bit of forgetting happen so that each review counts. Massed study (the same thing many times in one evening) feels productive because the material is still fresh, which is exactly why it builds less.

**How to do it.**

1. Learn the material once and make a few recall prompts (cards or questions).
2. Review after about a day, then after about 3 days, a week, two weeks and a month.
3. If you fail an item, shorten its next gap; if it was easy, lengthen it.
4. Let an app (Anki, or the flashcard tool you prefer) do the scheduling for large volumes. For whole topics, put review dates in your calendar.
5. Do the reviews due today before learning anything new.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A music student learning the key signatures reviews them on the bus on days 1, 3, 7 and 14, and by the end of the month names any key instantly.
- *Non-trivial:* A law student has an exam in eight weeks and a second one, built on the first, in a year. For the near exam, gaps of a few days to a week are best. For the material she needs in a year, she stretches later reviews to a month or more. Research suggests the best gap grows with how long you need to remember: roughly 10-20% of the time until the test for delays of weeks, and a smaller share (around 5-10%) when the test is a year away.

**⏱ Time cost.** About 5 short reviews of a paragraph over a month take \~2-3 minutes in total, spread out (\~4-6×). That is less than re-reading it twice a week, and it keeps the material for months rather than days.

**Watch out.**

- Letting reviews pile up. A missed week becomes hundreds of due cards. Short daily sessions beat a weekly marathon.
- Spacing without recall. Re-reading on day 7 is spaced, but it is not practice. Each review should be a closed-book attempt.
- Cards without understanding. Spacing keeps what you learned; it does not make sense of it. Understand first, then space.

### Blank-page recall (brain dump)

After a lecture or chapter, write or sketch everything you remember on a blank page, then fill the gaps from the source in another colour. It is a form of retrieval, ideal for same-day review and for finding gaps fast.

**Why it works.** A question gives you a cue; a blank page gives you none. You have to rebuild the structure of the topic yourself: what came first, what depends on what, what belongs together. That is the same organising work an exam essay or a real problem asks for. The second colour then makes your gaps visible at a glance, so you know exactly what to study next instead of re-reading everything.

**How to do it.**

1. Close everything. Take a blank sheet and write the topic at the top.
2. For 5-10 minutes, write or sketch all you remember: key points, links, diagrams, examples. Don't worry about order or neatness.
3. When you run dry, wait a minute. More usually comes.
4. Open the source and add what you missed or got wrong in a different colour.
5. The coloured parts are tomorrow's recall questions.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* After a biology lecture on the cell cycle, a student sketches the phases from memory and notices in red that she forgot what happens at the checkpoints.
- *Non-trivial:* A programmer learning a new web framework does a brain dump of "what happens between a request arriving and a page being sent back". His sketch has the right parts but in the wrong order, and the middleware step is missing. No single flashcard would have shown that, because each part on its own was "known". Blank-page recall tests the connections, not just the pieces.

**⏱ Time cost.** About 2 minutes for one paragraph (\~4×). For a whole lecture, 10-20 minutes plus the time to fill gaps.

**Watch out.**

- Doing it with notes in view. Even glancing turns it into copying.
- Filling gaps silently and moving on. The coloured items need to come back in later reviews, or the same gaps will reappear.
- Expecting it to be tidy. A messy page full of red is a successful session.

### Practice questions

Exam-style questions, then study the explanation for every wrong or guessed answer. They are best for applying knowledge, preparing for exams and finding weak topics.

**Why it works.** Knowing a fact and using it are different skills. A practice question makes you recognise which knowledge a situation calls for and apply it under constraints, which is what exams and real work ask. Wrong answers are the most valuable part. A mistake you made, followed quickly by the correct answer and the reason, is remembered better than a fact you simply read, because the error marks the exact spot where your understanding broke.

**How to do it.**

1. Use past papers, question banks, end-of-chapter problems, or questions you write from learning objectives.
2. Answer without notes, and commit to an answer before checking.
3. Mark each one: right and sure, right but guessed, or wrong.
4. For every guessed or wrong answer, read the explanation and write one line on why the right answer is right and why yours wasn't.
5. Keep an error log and redo those questions a few days later.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A physics student does five problems on Newton's second law and gets one wrong because she forgot to include friction. That single error teaches her more than the four she got right.
- *Non-trivial:* An economics student notices that most of her errors are on questions where she picks an answer that is true but doesn't answer what was asked. Her error log shows the problem is reading, not knowledge. Sorting mistakes by type (didn't know, misread, confused two ideas, ran out of time) tells you whether to study content, slow down, or practise timing.

**⏱ Time cost.** About 2-3 minutes per exam-style question, including reading the explanation (\~4-6×). More than a simple recall question, because it also trains application and exam technique.

**Watch out.**

- Saving questions for the last two weeks. Start early; they are a learning tool, not only a test.
- Chasing scores. Doing the same bank twice may raise the percentage through memory of the answers, not understanding.
- Skipping explanations for correct guesses. A lucky guess is a gap that hasn't shown itself yet.

### Interleaving

Mix related topics or problem types in one session instead of blocking one topic at a time. It works best for topics that are easy to confuse.

**Why it works.** When you practise in blocks (ten problems on the same method), the heading at the top of the page has already told you which method to use. You skip the hardest step of a real problem: deciding which strategy applies. Interleaving puts that decision back in. Each problem asks "what kind of problem is this?" before "how do I solve it?". It also places similar things side by side, so you notice the differences between them, which is exactly what you need when two ideas look alike. It feels slower and less fluent, and that is the point.

**How to do it.**

1. Learn each topic or method on its own first, so you have something to mix.
2. Build practice sets that mix 3-4 related types, in an unpredictable order.
3. Before solving each item, name its type ("this is integration by parts because…").
4. Prefer mixing things that are easy to confuse. Mixing unrelated subjects (French and chemistry) helps much less.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A French learner practises the passé composé and the imparfait in one mixed set of sentences instead of one tense per day, and has to decide each time which one fits.
- *Non-trivial:* An art history student studies paintings by six similar artists. Seeing one painting from each artist in turn, rather than all paintings by one artist and then the next, helps her identify new paintings later, even though she feels she learned better from the blocks. Your sense of how well a method worked is not a reliable judge here: blocking feels better and works worse.

**⏱ Time cost.** About 0 extra (\~1×). It changes the order of the work, not the amount, although it feels about 20-30% slower because each item is harder.

**Watch out.**

- Interleaving too early. If you can't yet do each type alone, mixing only causes confusion.
- Mixing unrelated topics. The benefit comes from having to discriminate between similar things.
- Giving up because scores dip. Performance during practice is lower; performance on the test later is higher.

### Elaborative interrogation

For each fact, ask "why is this true?" and "what happens if it fails?", and answer from your own knowledge. It turns isolated facts into connected understanding.

**Why it works.** An isolated fact has one thread holding it in memory; a fact you have explained is tied to everything you used to explain it. Asking "why?" forces you to connect the new fact to what you already know, and those connections become extra routes back to it later. It also exposes facts you have accepted without understanding. If you can't answer "why?", you have found a gap before the exam does.

**How to do it.**

1. Take one fact or claim from your notes.
2. Ask "why is this true?", "why here and not elsewhere?" or "what would happen if it were different?".
3. Answer in a sentence or two from your own knowledge, before looking anything up.
4. Check your answer against the source and fix it if needed.
5. Keep the best "why" questions as recall questions for later.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* "Water expands when it freezes." Why? Because the molecules lock into a hexagonal lattice that holds them further apart than in the liquid. What follows? Ice floats, so lakes freeze from the top and fish survive the winter.
- *Non-trivial:* A history student reads "the printing press helped the Reformation spread". Asking "why the printing press, and why then?" leads her to cheap pamphlets, rising literacy in towns, and the fact that Luther wrote in German, not Latin. One fact becomes a small causal network she can use in an essay. The richer your prior knowledge, the better the answers, so this technique grows more powerful as you go deeper into a subject.

**⏱ Time cost.** About 3-5 minutes for 2-3 "why?" questions on a paragraph (\~6-10×). Use it on the facts that matter most or feel arbitrary, not on every line.

**Watch out.**

- Accepting a vague answer ("because that's how it works"). If you can't explain it, look it up.
- Using it on genuinely arbitrary facts (a word's gender, a date). There is often no "why"; use mnemonics and spacing instead.
- Skipping the check. A confident wrong explanation is easy to remember too.

### Self-explanation

While studying an example or a figure, explain each step to yourself and how it links to what you know. It suits mechanisms, worked examples, proofs, code and pathways.

**Why it works.** Reading a worked solution is like watching someone drive: everything looks obvious until you take the wheel. Explaining each step to yourself makes you fill in what the author left out ("why did they divide by two here?"), and that is where the learning happens. It also exposes the difference between your mental model and the real one. When your explanation doesn't fit the next step, you have caught a misunderstanding while it is still cheap to fix.

**How to do it.**

1. Take a worked example, a proof, a diagram or a piece of code.
2. Go one step or line at a time. Before moving on, say or write: what this step does, why it is needed, and how it follows from the one before.
3. Link it to a rule or principle you already know ("this is the chain rule again").
4. When you can't explain a step, mark it and resolve it from the source or by asking.
5. Afterwards, try a similar problem without the example.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student follows a worked solution of a quadratic equation and, at each line, says what was done and why ("we complete the square so the left side becomes a perfect square").
- *Non-trivial:* A programming student reads a short sorting function and explains each line aloud. At the line that swaps two values she realises she can't say why the loop stops one element early. Working it out teaches her more about the algorithm than reading three textbook pages. The same works for a cardiac cycle diagram in physiology or a chord progression in music theory: the gap is where your explanation stalls.

**⏱ Time cost.** About 3-5 minutes per paragraph or worked example (\~6-10×). Most worthwhile for steps and mechanisms, where a misunderstanding would spread.

**Watch out.**

- Paraphrasing instead of explaining. "Then they add 3" describes; "they add 3 to both sides to keep the equation balanced" explains.
- Not checking. A wrong self-explanation can lock in a misconception. Compare with the source or a solution.
- Only reading worked examples. Self-explanation prepares you to solve; you still need to solve problems yourself.

### Feynman technique (explain it simply)

Explain a topic in plain words as if to a 12-year-old. Where you get stuck or use jargon you can't unpack is where your understanding stops.

**Why it works.** Most of us feel we understand things like zips, toilets or interest rates much better than we do. Researchers call this the illusion of explanatory depth: the feeling of understanding collapses the moment you have to spell out the mechanism step by step. Jargon is where the illusion hides, because a technical word can stand in for an explanation you don't actually have. Forcing yourself into plain words removes that hiding place, so the gaps become visible on the page.

**How to do it.**

1. Write the topic at the top of a blank page.
2. Explain it, book closed, in simple sentences and short words, as if to a bright child. Use an analogy where you can.
3. Mark every place where you hesitated, waved your hands or reached for a technical term.
4. Go back to the source for exactly those spots, then rewrite the explanation.
5. Read it aloud. If a sentence still sounds vague, simplify it once more.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Physics. "Why does a heavy ball and a light ball fall at the same speed?" You write "because gravity is the same" and stop: is the pull the same? No, the heavy ball is pulled harder, but it also needs a harder push to get moving. Those two cancel. That second sentence is the bit you didn't really have.
- *Non-trivial:* Economics. Explaining "the central bank raises interest rates to fight inflation" in plain words forces a chain: borrowing gets dearer, people and firms borrow and spend less, shops can't raise prices as easily. You stall at "why would that lower prices rather than just slow growth?", which is exactly the debate economists have. A gap you found is often the most interesting part of the topic.

**⏱ Time cost.** About 8-15 min for one paragraph's worth of ideas, roughly 15-30× one recall question (rough estimates). Use it for the central mechanisms of a course, not for every detail.

**Watch out.** Simple is not the same as vague: "the body just balances it" is not an explanation. And don't stop at writing; the gap-fixing in steps 3 and 4 is where the learning happens.

### Learning by teaching

Teach a classmate, a study group, or even an empty room. Preparing to teach also helps.

**Why it works.** Two separate effects stack up. First, *expecting* to teach changes how you study: when students were told they would have to teach a passage (and then only took a test), they recalled more of it, organised it better and remembered the main points better than students who studied for a test. You start looking for structure, the "what matters and in what order", instead of collecting facts. This is sometimes called the protégé effect: we work harder for someone else's understanding than for our own. Second, actually explaining means retrieving from memory and building a connected story in real time, and a listener's "wait, why?" exposes gaps that silent reading never does.

**How to do it.**

1. Study the topic as if you have to give a 10-minute lesson on it tomorrow.
2. Prepare a one-page outline: the 3-5 key ideas and one example each.
3. Teach it without notes, to a friend, a study group, a parent, or aloud to an empty room or a voice recorder.
4. Invite questions, or ask yourself the questions a confused learner would ask.
5. Afterwards, write down every point where you stumbled and fix it from the source.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Languages. You explain to a friend when Spanish uses *ser* and when *estar*. Their question "then why is it *está muerto*, if being dead is permanent?" shows that your "permanent vs temporary" rule is too crude.
- *Non-trivial:* Law. A study group splits a contract-law module: each person teaches one doctrine (offer and acceptance, consideration, misrepresentation) and brings two short problem cases. Preparing the cases forces you to find where the rule's edges are, and you learn the other doctrines through retrieval when you answer their questions.

**⏱ Time cost.** Teaching a paragraph's worth of material to a friend, including preparation, takes about 20-40 min, roughly 40-80× one recall question (rough estimates). In the same time you could answer 40-80 recall questions. So reserve teaching for the core ideas that everything else hangs on, and use cheap recall for the rest.

**Watch out.** Teaching without preparing turns into reading slides aloud, which is re-reading in disguise. And a polite audience that never asks questions gives you little; ask them to interrupt.

### Dual coding

Pair words with visuals: diagrams, flowcharts, timelines, sketches. Redraw them from memory.

**Why it works.** Memory stores verbal and visual information in partly separate ways. When an idea is held both as words and as a picture, you have two routes back to it, like a house with two doors: if one is locked, you can try the other. Drawing also forces decisions that text lets you skip, such as what comes first, what connects to what, and what is bigger.

**Why it is not "learning styles".** The claim that you learn best when teaching matches your preferred style ("I'm a visual learner") has not held up in well-designed tests. Dual coding is different: it says *everyone* benefits from combining words and meaningful visuals, and that the content decides the format. A map suits geography, a timeline suits history, and an argument is still best in words, whatever your preference.

**How to do it.**

1. Read a section, then close the book.
2. Turn it into a visual: a flowchart for a process, a timeline for events, a labelled sketch for a structure, a graph for a relationship.
3. Add short labels in words; the words and the picture should explain each other.
4. A few days later, redraw it from a blank page and compare.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* History. The causes of the First World War as a timeline with alliances drawn as lines between countries makes clear why one assassination pulled in so many states.
- *Non-trivial:* Programming. Drawing what happens in memory when you copy a list in Python (two arrows to the same box vs two boxes) explains a bug that a page of text about "references" never quite does. The value comes from drawing it yourself and then checking it.

**⏱ Time cost.** A quick sketch with labels takes about 5-10 min per paragraph, roughly 10-20× one recall question (rough estimates). Redrawing from memory later is the cheap, high-value part.

**Watch out.** Decorative pictures and colour-coding don't count; the visual must carry meaning. Copying a textbook diagram neatly is mostly copying, so redraw it from memory instead.

### Concrete examples

Link every abstract idea to a concrete case, example or story.

**Why it works.** Abstract ideas are hard to hold because there is nothing to grab. A concrete example hooks the new idea onto something you already know. But a single example has a hidden cost: you can't tell which of its features matter. Learn "opportunity cost" only from "the cinema ticket instead of the concert" and you may think it is about money or leisure. With two or three examples that look different on the surface but share the same structure, your brain is pushed to extract what they have in common, which is the concept itself. That is why several varied examples beat one.

**How to do it.**

1. For each abstract idea, find one example in your source.
2. Find or invent a second (and ideally a third) from a different context.
3. Say what the examples share and what is irrelevant about each.
4. Test yourself the other way round: given a new case, decide whether it is an example of the idea, and why.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Biology. "Negative feedback" = a thermostat switching the heating off when the room is warm enough. Second example: blood sugar rises, insulin is released, blood sugar falls. Now "the output turns down its own cause" is clear, and it is not about heating.
- *Non-trivial:* Economics and law. "Moral hazard": a person who drives less carefully after buying full car insurance; a bank that takes bigger risks because it expects a bailout; a tenant who doesn't care for a flat when the deposit is small. Three very different surfaces, one structure: someone protected from the cost of their behaviour takes more risk. A good check is a near-miss: someone who buys insurance because they already drive badly is a different idea (adverse selection).

**⏱ Time cost.** Finding or inventing two examples takes about 3-5 min per paragraph, roughly 6-10× one recall question (rough estimates). Worth it for any definition you will need to apply, not just recognise.

**Watch out.** Examples that are too similar teach the surface, not the idea. And check invented examples against the source; a wrong example teaches the wrong concept.

### Pretesting (errorful generation)

Try questions on a topic before you study it. Getting them wrong primes you to notice the answers.

**Why it works.** It sounds backwards: how can a test on something you haven't learned help? A failed attempt does two things. It opens a "slot" in your mind, a question you now care about, so when the answer arrives you notice it and it sticks. And your wrong guess activates related knowledge, so the right answer links to more of what you already know. It is like being told a riddle before hearing the punchline: the punchline lands. Experiments find that people who first guess and get it wrong often remember the material better than people who spent the same time just studying it, as long as they see the correct answer soon after.

**How to do it.**

1. Before a lecture, chapter or video, take 2-3 questions (from the end of the chapter, the learning objectives, a past paper, or your own guesses at what will be asked).
2. Answer each one in a few seconds. Guess freely; being wrong is the point.
3. Then study the material and look out for the answers.
4. Afterwards, check your guesses against the correct answers.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Languages. Before a lesson on German noun genders, guess the gender of *Mädchen* (girl). You will likely say feminine; it is neuter (*das Mädchen*), because every word ending in *-chen* is neuter. That surprise anchors the rule.
- *Non-trivial:* Medicine and statistics. Before a lecture on diagnostic tests, ask: "A test is 99% accurate and the disease affects 1 in 1,000 people. You test positive. How likely is it you have it?" Most people guess about 99%; the answer is about 9%. Having been wrong, you will follow the lecture's explanation of base rates closely, and you won't forget it.

**⏱ Time cost.** 2-3 questions before reading take about 1-2 min per paragraph, roughly 2-4× one recall question (rough estimates). One of the cheapest techniques here.

**Watch out.** The benefit depends on getting the correct answer soon afterwards; a wrong guess that is never corrected can stick. Keep it short and low-stakes, and don't let it replace testing after study.

### Successive relearning

Keep retrieving each item until you get it right, then come back on later days and do it again.

**Why it works.** It joins the two strongest techniques into one routine. Within a session you keep testing each item until you can produce it correctly (retrieval to a criterion), so nothing is left half-learned. Across sessions, spacing lets the memory fade a little, so each relearning takes effort and makes it stronger. A useful picture is a path through a field: walking it once leaves a trace that grows over; walking it again every few days, each time a bit more overgrown, turns it into a path that lasts. Relearning also gets faster each time, so the later sessions cost little. In classroom studies, students who used it remembered substantially more on course exams and weeks later.

**How to do it.**

1. Turn the material into questions or flashcards.
2. Session 1: go through them; anything you miss goes back into the pile until you answer it correctly once (some people use twice).
3. Come back after a gap, for example 2 days, then a week, then 2-3 weeks.
4. In each later session, again keep retrieving until each item is correct once.
5. Drop items that are quick and correct on the first try in two sessions running; keep the slow or missed ones.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Music. Learning the order of sharps for key signatures (F, C, G, D, A, E, B): recall it until correct today, then again on Thursday and next Monday. By the third session it takes seconds.
- *Non-trivial:* Maths. A list of 15 trigonometric identities and integration rules for an exam. In session 1 you need several rounds for some of them; by session 3 you get most right first time, and the ones that still fail tell you which rules you don't understand (not just haven't memorised), which is where to go back to the derivation.

**⏱ Time cost.** Recall to criterion now plus 3 later sessions takes about 3-4 min per paragraph in total, spread out, roughly 6-8× one recall question (rough estimates). More than one pass of recall, far less than restudying from scratch before the exam.

**Watch out.** Stopping at "I recognise it when I see the answer" is not reaching the criterion: produce it before you look. And don't squeeze all sessions into one week before the exam; the gaps are part of the method.

> - Article: [Push beyond "one and done"](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/archive/2018/successive-relearning), RetrievalPractice.org
> - Paper: Rawson, K. A., Dunlosky, J., & Sciartelli, S. M. (2013). [The power of successive relearning: improving performance on course exams and long-term retention](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-013-9240-4). *Educational Psychology Review*.

### A simple rule of thumb

Spend less time taking information in and more time getting it back out. A good target is at least half of study time spent on retrieval (questions, recall, cards) rather than reading or watching.

The reason is in the time-cost numbers above: one minute of retrieval usually buys more lasting memory than one minute of reading, and a session that is all input feels productive but leaves little behind a week later. Input is still needed; you can't recall what you never took in. The point is the balance.

Here is what that can look like in a 2-hour session on one new topic (say, a chapter on the French Revolution, a lecture on enzymes, or a unit on recursion):

| Time | Activity | Type |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0:00-0:05 | Due flashcard reviews from earlier topics | Retrieval |
| 0:05-0:10 | Pretest: 3 questions on today's chapter, guessing | Retrieval |
| 0:10-0:40 | Read or watch the new material once, with the pretest questions in mind | Input |
| 0:40-0:55 | Blank-page recall of what you just read, then check and fill gaps in another colour | Retrieval |
| 0:55-1:05 | Break | Rest |
| 1:05-1:20 | Clarify the gaps from the source; sketch one diagram or find two examples for the hardest idea | Input and elaboration |
| 1:20-1:45 | Practice questions mixing today's topic with last week's; read the explanation for every miss | Retrieval |
| 1:45-1:55 | Explain the one central idea aloud in plain words (a short Feynman pass) | Retrieval |
| 1:55-2:00 | Make 5-10 flashcards for facts you missed, to come back in successive relearning | Planning |

That is about 60 minutes of retrieval against 45 of input and elaboration, a little over half of the 110 working minutes. Notice where the expensive techniques go: one diagram, two examples and one plain-words explanation, all spent on the central idea, while the cheap techniques (cards, recall, questions) carry the rest.

## Note-taking and understanding

Good notes are a tool for testing yourself later, not a copy of the lecture, the book or the video. Keep them short, in your own words, and built around questions.

The useful question about any note format is not "does it look complete?" but "what will I *do* with this page next week?" Notes you can quiz yourself from, redraw from memory or compare across topics keep paying back. Notes you can only re-read pay back very little. The time costs below use the same scale as the rest of this compendium (one recall question of about 30 seconds = 1×); they are rough estimates from typical practice, not measured values.

### Cornell notes

Split the page into a narrow left column and a wide right column. Notes go on the right; afterwards, write questions on the left. Review by covering the right side and answering.

**Why it works.** The page layout builds the test into the notes. Writing the notes is the cheap, passive part; writing the cue questions forces you to decide what each chunk is *for*, and covering the right column turns every later review into retrieval practice instead of re-reading. Think of it as notes with the answer key folded behind them.

**How to do it.**

1. Draw a vertical line about a third of the way across the page, and leave 4-5 lines free at the bottom.
2. During the class or reading, write brief notes in the wide right column. Skip lines between ideas.
3. Within a day, write one question or cue word in the left column next to each chunk.
4. At the bottom, write a two- or three-sentence summary of the page in your own words.
5. To review, cover the right column, answer each question aloud or on scrap paper, then uncover and check.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A school history lesson on the causes of the First World War. Left column: "What were the four long-term causes (MAIN)?" Right column: militarism, alliances, imperialism, nationalism, each with one detail.
- *Non-trivial:* An introductory economics lecture on elasticity. Instead of cues that only ask for definitions ("What is price elasticity?"), write cues that force use: "Why does a tax on cigarettes raise revenue but barely cut smoking?" The answer requires the definition *and* the idea that demand for addictive goods is inelastic, so one cue tests two layers.

**⏱ Time cost.** About +2-4 min per paragraph's worth of notes over plain notes (\~4-8×), most of it in writing the cue questions.

**Watch out.** Writing the questions and never covering the right column again gives you the cost without the benefit. Cues that only name a topic ("Elasticity") are weaker than real questions.

### Question-based notes

Write each heading as a question ("How does the kidney concentrate urine?", "Why did the Weimar Republic collapse?", "When should I use a hash map instead of a list?"). The notes become a quiz you made yourself.

**Why it works.** A statement heading ("Weimar Republic") tells you where you are; a question heading tells you what you are supposed to be able to *do*. While you take notes, the question acts like a filter: anything that does not help answer it is probably a detail. Later, the headings alone are a ready-made test. It is the same idea as Cornell notes with the questions moved to the top.

**How to do it.**

1. Before the class or chapter, turn each section title into a "how", "why" or "what happens if" question.
2. Take notes under each question, aiming to answer it in a few lines.
3. Add new questions when the lecturer or author raises something you did not expect.
4. To review, read only the headings and answer each one before looking at the notes underneath.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A biology chapter heading "Photosynthesis" becomes "Where does the oxygen released in photosynthesis come from?"
- *Non-trivial:* In a contract law course, the topic "Offer and acceptance" becomes several questions that mirror how exam problems are set: "When does an advert count as an offer?", "When is an emailed acceptance effective?", "What happens if the reply changes a term?" Each question is a small problem, so the notes double as practice for problem questions, not just for definitions.

**⏱ Time cost.** About +2-4 min per paragraph's worth of notes over plain notes (\~4-8×). Turning headings into questions before class takes only a minute or two per lecture.

**Watch out.** "What is X?" questions slide back into definitions. Mix in "why", "how" and "compare" questions.

### Annotate the slides instead of rewriting them

When the lecture slides (or a handout, textbook chapter or set of lecture notes) are the spine, add your notes, links and "why" answers directly on them rather than copying them out.

**Why it works.** Copying out slides costs a lot of time and attention, and most of it goes into handwriting, not thinking. If the content is already on the page, your scarce attention in a lecture is better spent on what the slides leave out: the lecturer's explanation, the example, the "this is the bit people get wrong". An experiment on slide handouts found that students who had the handouts during the lecture took fewer notes but did at least as well on later tests as students who had to write more.

**How to do it.**

1. Get the slides or handout before the class (PDF on a tablet, or printed).
2. During the class, write only what is *not* on the slide: the reason, the example, the link to an earlier topic, the lecturer's hints about the exam.
3. After the class, add a question in the margin of each important slide.
4. Mark slides you do not understand with a symbol and fix them within a few days.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A statistics slide gives the formula for standard deviation. You write next to it: "why divide by n-1? Because the sample mean is closer to the data than the true mean, so we would underestimate spread."
- *Non-trivial:* A first-year law lecture uses slides that list case names and holdings. Rather than copy the holdings, you annotate each case with a one-line fact pattern and an arrow to the case it distinguishes or overrules. The slides now show the *line* of reasoning between cases, which is what problem questions test and what the bare list could not show.

**⏱ Time cost.** About the cost of highlighting (\~1-1.5 min per paragraph-sized slide, \~2-3×), plus about one recall question's worth (\~30 s, 1×) for each "why" you add. Rewriting the slide content by hand costs several times more and adds little.

**Watch out.** Annotating can drift into colourful highlighting of what is already there. If an annotation does not add a reason, an example or a question, it is decoration.

### Concept maps

Boxes and labelled arrows (causes, inhibits, leads to, is an example of) show how ideas connect. Redraw them from memory a few days later.

**Why it works.** Linear notes store ideas in the order you met them; a concept map stores them in the order they *relate*. The labels on the arrows are where the learning happens, because writing "raises", "requires" or "contradicts" forces you to state the relationship rather than just place two ideas near each other. Redrawing the map from memory adds retrieval: in one well-known experiment, students who practised recall learned more than students who built concept maps with the text open, and later work found that building the map *from memory* works about as well as written recall.

**How to do it.**

1. List the 8-15 key ideas of a topic.
2. Put the most general idea in the middle or at the top.
3. Connect ideas with arrows, and write a verb or short phrase on every arrow.
4. Look for cross-links between distant parts of the map; these are often the insights.
5. A few days later, redraw it on a blank page without looking, then compare and fix it in another colour.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* An ecology map: "sunlight → (powers) → plants → (eaten by) → herbivores → (eaten by) → predators", with "decomposers → (return nutrients to) → soil → (feeds) → plants" closing the loop.
- *Non-trivial:* A map of the 2008 financial crisis for an economics or history course. Drawing it reveals a feedback loop (falling house prices → mortgage defaults → bank losses → less lending → falling house prices) that a linear list of causes hides. Finding the loop yourself is the moment you understand why the crisis spread.

**⏱ Time cost.** About 10-15 min for a map drawn from memory (\~20-30× one recall question). Worth it for the central ideas of a topic, not for every paragraph.

**Watch out.** Arrows without labels make a pretty picture that tests nothing. Copying a map from the book is much weaker than building or redrawing your own.

### Flowcharts and pathway sketches

For anything that unfolds as a chain of steps or of cause and effect, draw the chain: a historical sequence of causes, an algorithm, a legal procedure, a chemical or metabolic pathway, the course of a disease, a supply-and-demand shock.

**Why it works.** Processes are hard to hold in words because each step depends on the one before. A sketch lets you see the whole chain at once and spot where a branch, a loop or a missing step is. Drawing also gives the memory two routes in, a verbal one and a visual one (dual coding). And a sketch is easy to test: cover one box and ask what goes there, or ask "what happens downstream if this step fails?"

**How to do it.**

1. Write the start and the end of the process first.
2. Fill in the steps between them as boxes, with arrows for "leads to".
3. Mark decision points (diamonds or "if...then") and feedback loops.
4. Add one short label per arrow explaining *why* one step leads to the next.
5. Later, redraw it from memory, then predict what happens if you break one link.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A programming exercise: before coding a login form, sketch the flow "enter password → check against stored hash → match? → yes: open session / no: count attempts → 3 failures? → lock account".
- *Non-trivial:* The path to the French Revolution drawn as a chain: war debts → fiscal crisis → Estates-General called → dispute over voting → Third Estate declares National Assembly → Bastille. Sketching it shows that the chain has a *trigger* (fiscal crisis) sitting on top of slower conditions (social inequality, Enlightenment ideas), and that the same trigger without those conditions might have produced only a reform. In biochemistry the same habit makes glycolysis or the Krebs cycle learnable: ask which step uses ATP, which produces it, and what happens to the whole pathway if one enzyme is blocked.

**⏱ Time cost.** About 5-10 min for a quick sketch with labels (\~10-20× one recall question), similar to other dual-coding work.

**Watch out.** Spending your time on neat shapes and colours instead of the arrow labels. A messy sketch you can redraw from memory beats a beautiful one you cannot.

### Comparison tables

For things that are easy to confuse, make a table of distinguishing features: types of anaemia, antibiotic classes, verb tenses, rival economic theories, sorting algorithms, painters of one period, similar legal offences.

**Why it works.** Confusable things are rarely forgotten one by one; they get mixed up with each other. Studying them separately never forces you to notice the difference. A table puts them side by side on the same features, so your attention goes exactly to what tells them apart. Research on comparing cases finds a reliable benefit from comparison over studying cases one at a time.

**How to do it.**

1. Put the confusable items as columns (3-6 is a good size).
2. Choose rows that *discriminate*: the features you would use to tell them apart in an exam question.
3. Fill in the table, then highlight the one feature in each row that is unique to one item.
4. To review, cover one column and reconstruct it, or cover the column headings and identify the item from its features.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Spanish past tenses: preterite vs imperfect, with rows "completed or ongoing?", "signal words", "typical use", "example sentence".
- *Non-trivial:* Sorting algorithms in a computer science course: bubble sort, merge sort, quicksort, heap sort, with rows "average time", "worst case", "extra memory", "stable?", "when to choose it". The non-obvious insight comes from the rows, not the columns: quicksort is usually fastest in practice yet has the worst worst-case, so the table shows you *why* "it depends" is the right answer and what it depends on. In medicine the same layout separates types of anaemia by cell size, iron studies and typical cause.

**⏱ Time cost.** About 10-20 min per table (\~20-40×), but one table usually covers several paragraphs or a whole chapter.

**Watch out.** Rows that list everything rather than what discriminates make a long table you will not review. Comparing similarities matters too, so add a row for what the items share.

### One-page summaries

At the end of a topic, condense it onto one page. The act of choosing what matters is itself learning.

**Why it works.** A page limit is a forcing function. You cannot keep everything, so you have to rank ideas, merge details under principles and decide what the topic is really about. That ranking is understanding. But be honest about the evidence: summarising, as students usually do it (shortening the text while looking at it), was rated a low-utility technique in a major review. The benefit comes from making the summary *from memory* and from the decisions about what to leave out, not from producing a neat page.

**How to do it.**

1. Close the book and notes.
2. On one page, write the topic's 3-5 main ideas and how they connect (a mini-map or table is fine).
3. Only then open your notes, and add what you missed in a different colour. The added parts are your weak spots.
4. Use the page later for blank-page recall: try to reproduce it before looking.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* One page on the water cycle: four processes, one diagram, one sentence on what drives it (energy from the sun).
- *Non-trivial:* One page on a whole term of introductory microeconomics. The page limit forces you to notice that most chapters are applications of the same two or three ideas (marginal thinking, supply and demand, incentives), so the summary becomes a structure rather than a list. For a long literature course, the same exercise might be one page per *theme* across several novels rather than one per book.

**⏱ Time cost.** About 15-30 min per topic (\~30-60×). Expensive, so keep it for the end of a topic or a revision checkpoint.

**Watch out.** Writing the summary with the book open turns it into copying. Pages that are too dense to recall from defeat the purpose.

### Longhand vs laptop

Handwriting forces you to summarise rather than transcribe, which may help understanding. A tablet with a stylus works the same way. If you type, paraphrase instead of transcribing.

**Why it works.** The idea comes from a 2014 study in which students who took notes by hand did better on conceptual questions than students who typed; the typists wrote more words and copied more of the lecture verbatim. The explanation was intuitive: fast typing lets you transcribe without thinking, slow writing forces you to choose. But the evidence is mixed. A careful replication in 2019 did not find the conceptual advantage, and a large direct replication in 2021 found only small, non-significant differences. The most defensible conclusion is that the *device* matters less than *what you do with it*: processing and paraphrasing helps, verbatim transcription does not.

**How to do it.**

1. Choose the tool that suits the class: handwriting or a stylus for maths, diagrams and formulas; typing for fast, text-heavy content or when you need searchable notes.
2. Whatever you use, write in your own words and in fragments, not full sentences copied from the speaker.
3. If you type, switch off notifications and other tabs; the distraction cost of a laptop is well documented even where the handwriting effect is not.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* In a physics lecture, handwritten notes make it easy to sketch force diagrams and write equations; typing them would be slow and clumsy.
- *Non-trivial:* In a fast history or philosophy seminar, a student types, but with a rule: no sentence longer than ten words, and every third line is a "so what?" comment. The notes end up shorter than a typical laptop transcript and closer to what the handwriting studies say helps, which shows that the active ingredient is the processing, not the pen.

**⏱ Time cost.** \~0 extra (\~1×). It changes the medium, not the amount of study time.

**Watch out.** Treating "handwriting is better" as settled science. Also, a tablet full of open apps carries the same distraction risk as a laptop.

### Pre-reading (priming)

Spend 10 to 15 minutes skimming headings, figures and learning objectives before a lecture, a class or a long reading. You will follow it better and know where the hard parts are.

**Why it works.** Arriving cold, you spend the first half of a lecture building a rough map of the topic, and details slide past before there is anywhere to put them. A quick skim builds the map in advance, like looking at a route before driving it. It works even better if you try to answer a few questions before the class: research on pretesting and prequestions shows that even *failed* attempts to answer improve later learning of the material, because they make you notice the answer when it comes.

**How to do it.**

1. Skim the headings, the figures and their captions, the summary and any objectives. Do not read in depth.
2. Write 2-3 questions you expect the class to answer, or try the end-of-chapter questions and guess.
3. Note the one part that looks hardest; that is where your attention should peak in class.
4. After the class, check whether your questions were answered.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Before a chemistry lecture on acids and bases, you look at the slides' headings and try to answer "Why is a weak acid weak?" You get it wrong, and the lecturer's answer sticks because you were waiting for it.
- *Non-trivial:* Before a seminar on a 40-page reading in sociology or political theory, you spend 15 minutes on the introduction, the conclusion and the first sentence of each section. Now you read the full text knowing the argument's destination, so you can tell which sections are the argument and which are evidence or digression. The same skim before a music theory class might be playing through the piece once while looking at the score.

**⏱ Time cost.** About 10-15 min per lecture. For a single paragraph, trying 2-3 questions before reading costs \~1-2 min (\~2-4×, like pretesting).

**Watch out.** Pre-reading that turns into a full first read leaves no time for review afterwards. Keep it a skim.

### Learning objectives as a checklist

Many courses publish what you are expected to learn: learning objectives per lecture, a syllabus, exam board specifications, course or module outcomes, or a certification's exam guide. Turn each one into a question and make sure you can answer it with the book closed.

**Why it works.** The objectives are the closest thing you have to a list of what the exam is written from. Read as statements ("Students will understand X"), they slide past; turned into questions, they become a self-test that covers the whole course with no gaps. A 2020 study found that learning objectives worked much better when students met them as pretest questions than as statements.

**How to do it.**

1. Copy the objectives or specification points into a list.
2. Rewrite each one as a question, using its verb: "explain" becomes "why/how", "compare" becomes a table, "calculate" becomes a worked problem.
3. Answer each from memory, then mark it green (solid), amber (shaky) or red (cannot answer).
4. Revisit amber and red items first; recheck the whole list before the exam.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A school chemistry specification point, "describe the test for carbon dioxide", becomes "What is the test for CO2 and what result do you see?" Thirty seconds, done.
- *Non-trivial:* A university statistics module outcome says "choose and justify an appropriate test for a given research design". Turning that into one question is not enough; the verb "choose and justify" means you need a set of mini-scenarios ("two groups, non-normal data, small n") and must name and defend the test for each. The objective tells you not just *what* to know but *how* you will be asked to use it. The same reading of verbs works for a language exam ("can describe past events" means producing past tenses, not reciting rules).

**⏱ Time cost.** About one recall question per objective (\~30 s, 1×) for simple ones; objectives that ask you to apply or compare take as long as a practice question (\~2-3 min, \~4-6×).

**Watch out.** Ticking an objective because you recognise the words. Only a closed-book answer counts.

### Don't over-polish notes

Beautiful, colour-coded notes can become a form of procrastination. Notes are only as good as how often you test yourself from them.

**Why it works.** Polishing feels like studying because it is effortful and produces something visible. But the effort goes into lettering, layout and colours, not into retrieving or connecting ideas. Learning research distinguishes passive activities (re-reading, copying, highlighting) from generative ones (explaining, mapping, testing yourself); copying neatly is near the passive end, however long it takes. It is like spending your gym hour arranging the weights.

**How to do it.**

1. Set a time limit for making notes (for example, no more than half the time you spend testing yourself from them).
2. Use colour only when it carries meaning (red for "I got this wrong", a consistent colour for causes vs effects), not for decoration.
3. Accept messy first drafts. If a page needs to be clean, make it clean in the second pass, from memory.
4. Check your ratio each week: hours making notes vs hours answering questions from them.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student spends 40 minutes rewriting a biology chapter in four colours. The same 40 minutes could have been about 80 recall questions on the chapter.
- *Non-trivial:* A language learner keeps an elegant vocabulary notebook with calligraphy headings and drawings. It is pleasant, and that motivation is real, so the fix is not to ban it. The fix is to make the notebook *testable*: fold each page so the target-language column is hidden, and use it for self-tests on the bus. The beauty stays; the notebook now does work.

**⏱ Time cost.** Polishing a page of notes costs about as much as a one-page summary (\~15-30 min, \~30-60×) while giving much less. Twenty minutes of decorating is roughly forty recall questions you did not answer.

**Watch out.** Over-correcting into notes so terse that you cannot understand them a week later. The goal is useful, not ugly.

### Which note format for which material?

No single format is best. Match the format to the *shape* of the material: is it a set of questions, a web of relations, a sequence, a set of look-alikes, or a big picture?

| Shape of the material | Best format | Examples from different fields |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Facts and definitions you must recall on demand | Cornell or question-based notes | Vocabulary and grammar rules in a language course; dates and treaties in history; definitions in statistics; drug names in pharmacology |
| Ideas that influence each other (a web) | Concept map | Causes of the 2008 crisis in economics; food webs in ecology; themes and characters in a novel; hormones and feedback in physiology |
| Steps in order, causes and effects | Flowchart or process sketch | An algorithm in programming; a civil or criminal procedure in law; the build-up to a revolution in history; a metabolic pathway in biochemistry; the stages of a chemical synthesis |
| Look-alikes that are easy to confuse | Comparison table | Verb tenses in Spanish or French; sorting algorithms in computer science; rival schools of thought in philosophy or economics; types of anaemia in medicine; similar offences in criminal law |
| A lecture that already has good slides | Annotated slides | Most lecture-based university courses: physics, psychology, engineering, medicine |
| A whole topic before an exam | One-page summary from memory | End of a unit in geography, a term of microeconomics, a module of organic chemistry, a period in art history |
| What the exam will ask | Objectives turned into questions | School exam specifications; university module outcomes; professional certification exam guides (IT, accounting, medical licensing) |

A practical rule: use the cheap formats (question-based or Cornell notes, annotated slides) for every class, and save the expensive ones (maps, tables, one-page summaries) for the 10-20% of ideas the rest of the topic hangs on or the places where you keep getting confused. Whatever the format, the notes earn their time only when you close them and test yourself.

## Memory techniques for high-volume facts

Every field has a large amount of material that has no logic of its own: vocabulary and grammar exceptions, dates, article numbers in a legal code, formulas and constants, anatomy, drug side effects, chord shapes. Understanding helps you organise it, but in the end you simply have to hold it. For that material, spaced flashcards plus a few memory tricks work best.

### Anki and flashcards done well

Make cards within 24 hours of the lecture or chapter. Do reviews every day before adding new cards. Keep one idea per card. Use cloze deletions (fill-in-the-blank) for facts and image occlusion for diagrams. This applies to any spaced-repetition app (Anki, SuperMemo, RemNote, Mochi and others), not only to Anki.

**Why it works.** A spaced-repetition app is a scheduler for active recall: it shows you each card just before you would forget it, and pushes cards you know well further into the future. That puts your time where forgetting is about to happen, which is why a few minutes a day can hold thousands of facts. The catch is that the app only schedules; the quality of what you remember depends on how the card is written. A vague card is like a key cut slightly wrong: it sometimes opens the lock, and you never know why.

**How to do it.**

1. Make cards the same day, while the material still makes sense to you.
2. Write each card so it has one clear, short answer. If a card asks for a list of seven things, split it or turn it into overlapping clozes.
3. Do your due reviews first every day. New cards only after reviews are done; a backlog of reviews is how most people quit.
4. Use cloze deletions for facts in context, image occlusion for diagrams and maps, and reversed cards only where you need both directions (word to meaning, meaning to word).
5. Add a little context to the front ("Contract law:", "Python lists:") so similar cards do not get confused.
6. Delete or rewrite any card you keep failing. Repeated failure usually means a bad card, not a bad memory.

Two short guides are worth reading once before you write many cards: Piotr Wozniak's "20 rules of formulating knowledge" (understand before you memorise, keep items minimal, avoid sets and enumerations) and Andy Matuschak's prompt-writing guide (good prompts are focused, precise, consistent, and require real retrieval rather than guessing).

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A French learner turns "la voiture (f.), the car" into a cloze card: "{{c1::La}} voiture est rouge." The card tests the gender in a real sentence, which is what she actually gets wrong when speaking.
- *Non-trivial:* A law student first writes "What are the elements of negligence?" and keeps failing it, because it asks for four things at once. She rewrites it as four cards ("Negligence: after duty of care, the claimant must show a {{c1::breach}} of that duty", and so on) plus one card that asks "Which element fails when the harm was not reasonably foreseeable?" The small cards hold the facts; the last card makes her use them.

**⏱ Time cost.** Making 2-4 cards for a paragraph takes \~3-5 min (\~6-10×); each later review takes \~10-20 s (\~0.5×). The card-making is the expensive part, so only card what you will really need. These are rough estimates, not measurements.

**Watch out.**

- Adding new cards every day until reviews take two hours. Set a daily cap for new cards (for example 10-20) and keep it.
- Cards that can be answered by recognising the shape of the question instead of recalling the answer.
- Copying whole sentences from the book. If a card is longer than two lines, it is probably two cards.

### Premade decks

Shared decks save hours, but only if they match your course content. Turn on (unsuspend) just the cards that match what you are currently studying instead of the whole deck. Examples include the AnKing deck for US medical licensing exams, frequency-ordered vocabulary decks for languages, decks built to follow a particular textbook, and community decks for things like country capitals, the periodic table or kanji.

**Why it works.** Making cards is the most expensive step of spaced repetition, so a good deck written by someone else hands you hundreds of hours of work for free. But you pay in a different coin. When you write a card yourself you have already thought about the fact once (the generation effect), and the card fits the way your course frames it. A premade card arrives cold: it may be correct and still feel like a stranger's sentence. The best use is a hybrid: borrow the wording, but only for what you have just learned.

**How to do it.**

1. Check fit first: compare 20 random cards with your syllabus or textbook. If many are irrelevant or framed differently, the deck will cost more than it saves.
2. Suspend everything, then unsuspend by tag or chapter as you cover each topic in class.
3. Edit freely. Add your lecturer's example, your own mnemonic or a picture to cards that will not stick.
4. Write your own cards for anything the deck misses, especially what your lecturer stressed.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A Japanese learner uses a shared kanji deck but unlocks only the 15 kanji from this week's textbook chapter, so reviews and lessons reinforce each other.
- *Non-trivial:* A medical student on the AnKing deck notices her own course teaches a topic differently from the US exam the deck was built for. She keeps the deck's cards for shared basics, suspends the ones on content her exams do not test, and adds 10 own cards with her lecturer's framing. The deck becomes a base, not a syllabus.

**⏱ Time cost.** You skip most of the card-making cost (\~3-5 min, \~6-10× per paragraph) and pay only for reviews (\~10-20 s, \~0.5× per card) plus a few minutes per week to unsuspend and edit. Rough estimates.

**Watch out.**

- Turning on the whole deck at once. Thousands of new cards on topics you have not met are just noise, and the review pile becomes unmanageable.
- Learning a deck instead of a course. If your exam is written by your lecturer, the deck is a helper, not the spine.

### Don't let cards replace understanding

Learn the mechanism or the reasoning first, then card the details. A card you can't explain is a card you will forget.

**Why it works.** A fact you understand is held in place by many threads: what causes it, what it causes, what it is similar to. A fact learned alone hangs on one thread, the card itself, and that thread snaps under exam pressure or when the question is worded differently. Understanding also tells you which details matter. People given a confusing passage remember far less of it than people who first see what it is about, even though the words are identical.

**How to do it.**

1. Study the topic first: explain it to yourself, draw it, or do a few problems.
2. Only then make cards, and make them for the details that hang on the structure you now see.
3. Add at least one "why" or "what follows" card per topic, not only "what" cards.
4. When a card keeps failing, go back to the source and ask what you did not understand, before rewriting the card.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* In chemistry, "Group 1 metals react violently with water" is easy to card. Knowing they have one outer electron they give up easily makes the card almost unnecessary, and also tells you why reactivity increases down the group.
- *Non-trivial:* A programming student has cards for 30 Git commands and still panics at merge conflicts. After spending an hour on how Git stores commits as a graph, half the commands become obvious consequences, and her remaining cards ("What does rebase do to the commit graph?") are fewer and better.

**⏱ Time cost.** The understanding step costs about as much as self-explanation or elaborative interrogation: \~3-5 min per paragraph (\~6-10×). It usually pays back by needing fewer cards and fewer failed reviews. Rough estimates.

**Watch out.** Using "I need to understand it first" as a reason never to start memorising. Some material (vocabulary, article numbers, chord names) has nothing to understand; card it directly.

### Mnemonics and acronyms

Useful for arbitrary lists like the cranial nerves, the order of the planets, the causes of a condition or the notes on a stave. Make them silly, vivid and personal. Commercial versions such as Sketchy and Pixorize use picture stories for subjects like microbiology and pharmacology.

**Why it works.** A mnemonic does not make the facts meaningful; it borrows meaning from something you already know well and hangs the facts on it. Your memory is good at vivid, odd, emotional images and at sentences, and poor at strings of unrelated items. A mnemonic translates the second into the first. It works like a luggage tag: the bag (the knowledge) is still yours to pack, but the tag makes it findable.

**How to do it.**

1. Use mnemonics only for material without its own logic: lists, orders, word-to-meaning pairs.
2. Pick the type: an acronym (the first letters spell a word), an acrostic (a sentence whose words start with the letters), or a keyword image (a sound-alike word linked to the meaning).
3. Make it vivid, absurd or personal. "My Very Educated Mother Just Served Us Nachos" is remembered because it is a little odd.
4. Test the mnemonic, then the facts behind it. Make a card for both.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Music students learn the treble-clef lines E, G, B, D, F with "Every Good Boy Deserves Fruit."
- *Non-trivial:* A Spanish learner uses the keyword method for "pato" (duck): she imagines a duck being patted on the head. It works for a few hundred hard words, but she notices the image fades once she has used the word in conversation a few times. That is the ideal outcome: the mnemonic was scaffolding, and the real memory has replaced it.

**⏱ Time cost.** \~2-5 min to build a mnemonic for one list (\~4-10×). Cheap for a list you need for years, expensive if you make one for every fact. Rough estimates.

**Watch out.**

- A mnemonic for something you could understand instead. It gives you the words without the meaning.
- Too many similar mnemonics. Ten acrostics about ten lists start to blur into each other.

### Memory palace (method of loci)

Place items along a route through a familiar place, such as your home. It works well for ordered lists.

**Why it works.** Human memory for places is very strong: you can walk through your home in your mind and never miss a room. The memory palace uses that existing map as a set of hooks. Each item becomes a vivid image left at a fixed spot, and walking the route gives you the items back in order. Memory champions use this almost without exception, and ordinary people who trained it for six weeks more than doubled their recall of word lists.

**How to do it.**

1. Choose a place you know very well and a fixed route through it (front door, hall, kitchen, sofa, window...).
2. Turn each item into a concrete, exaggerated image.
3. Place one image at each spot and make it interact with the spot (it is melting on the sofa, singing in the sink).
4. Walk the route in your mind a few times, then recall without looking.
5. Review it like any other material, spaced over days.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* To remember a shopping list, you picture a giant egg blocking the front door, milk flooding the hall and bread used as a pillow on the sofa.
- *Non-trivial:* A history student needs the order of the English monarchs for an essay exam. She puts each one along her daily walk to campus, with an image for the key event of the reign at each landmark. Because the route has an order, she can also answer "who came after Henry VII?" by stepping forward one place, and the event images give her dates and context for free.

**⏱ Time cost.** \~10-20 min to set up a 10-item list (\~20-40×). Expensive, so keep it for ordered lists you must hold exactly: speeches, sequences, steps of a procedure. Rough estimates.

**Watch out.**

- Using it for material that needs understanding. A palace stores order and names, not reasons.
- Reusing the same palace for many lists too soon. Old images interfere with new ones; use several routes.

### Chunking

Group items into meaningful clusters (for example, drugs by mechanism, verbs by conjugation pattern, cases by legal principle) instead of memorising them one by one.

**Why it works.** Working memory can only juggle a handful of items at once, but an "item" can be large if it is meaningful. A chess master does not see 25 pieces; she sees four or five familiar patterns. A phone number is easier as 0207 946 0123 than as ten digits. Chunking turns many small things into a few big ones, and the grouping itself carries information: once you know the group, each member is half remembered already.

**How to do it.**

1. Lay out the whole list in front of you.
2. Look for a meaningful rule that sorts it: same mechanism, same ending, same period, same cause.
3. Name each group and learn the rule first, then the members.
4. Learn the exceptions separately and on purpose; they are where exam questions live.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Instead of 20 German prepositions one by one, you learn them as four groups by the case they take (accusative, dative, two-way, genitive). One rule now covers five words at a time.
- *Non-trivial:* An economics student memorises 30 historical financial crises one by one and keeps confusing them. She regroups them by cause (bank runs, currency pegs breaking, asset bubbles, sovereign debt). The groups make her see a pattern she can use in an essay, and the odd ones that fit no group turn out to be the most interesting ones.

**⏱ Time cost.** \~2-5 min to group a list (\~4-10×). Often the best value of all memory tricks, because it also builds understanding. Rough estimates.

**Watch out.** Groups that are arbitrary (alphabetical, by lecture number) do not help much. The grouping should be one you would want to know anyway.

### Story and case links

Attach facts to a case, a character, a story or a concrete scenario. "A 45-year-old with fatigue and a smooth tongue" holds B12 deficiency together better than a list does; a single court case holds a legal rule better than the rule alone.

**Why it works.** Stories have a structure your memory already expects: someone wants something, something happens, there is a cause and a consequence. Each part of a story cues the next, so remembering one detail pulls the others along. A list has no such links. In a classic experiment, people who made up a story to link lists of words recalled several times more of them than people who simply rehearsed the lists.

**How to do it.**

1. For a cluster of facts, find or invent one case that contains them all: a patient, a client, a historical person, a bug in a program.
2. Give it a few concrete details (an age, a place, a problem).
3. Tell it in order: what happened, why, what followed.
4. Recall the story from memory, then pull the facts back out of it.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A geography student remembers the features of a Mediterranean climate through one imagined week in a Greek village: dry hot summer, olive harvest, rain only in winter.
- *Non-trivial:* A law student learns the rules of offer and acceptance through the story of one person selling a car to a neighbour: the advert (an invitation to treat), the letter of acceptance that gets lost in the post (the postal rule), the revocation that arrives too late. One story now holds five rules in the order a problem question tests them.

**⏱ Time cost.** About the same as finding concrete examples: \~3-5 min per cluster (\~6-10×). One good story can cover several paragraphs. Rough estimates.

**Watch out.** A story that gets the facts slightly wrong will be remembered just as well as a correct one. Check it against the source.

### Spaced reviews for whole topics too

Not everything needs a flashcard. Reviewing a whole topic by blank-page recall on day 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 covers understanding as well as facts.

**Why it works.** Flashcards test pieces; exams and real use ask you to put pieces together. Writing out a whole topic from memory tests the structure: what comes first, what causes what, where each detail belongs. Spacing those attempts gives the same benefit as spaced flashcards, applied to the whole map instead of single facts. Think of cards as checking the bricks and whole-topic recall as checking the building.

**How to do it.**

1. After first learning a topic, schedule reviews at growing gaps (for example 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days).
2. At each review, take a blank page and write or sketch everything you remember about the topic for 10-15 minutes.
3. Check against your notes and fill the gaps in another colour.
4. If a review went well, stretch the next gap; if it went badly, shorten it.
5. Turn the facts you keep missing into flashcards.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A physics student reviews "circular motion" by writing the key equations and drawing the force diagrams from memory on day 1, 3 and 7.
- *Non-trivial:* A history student studying the French Revolution finds she can answer all her date cards but cannot write a coherent paragraph on why the monarchy fell. Whole-topic recall on day 7 shows the problem: she knows the events but not the causal chain. She adds a timeline with arrows to her review and lets the date cards run on their own.

**⏱ Time cost.** Each review is a blank-page recall: \~2 min per paragraph's worth (\~4×). Five reviews over a month come to \~10 min per paragraph in total, spread out, though in practice a one-page dump covers a whole topic in outline and is cheaper per paragraph. Rough estimates.

**Watch out.**

- Reviewing by re-reading notes instead of writing from memory. That is the low-value version.
- Losing track of what is due. Keep a simple list or calendar of topics and review dates.

## Managing many sources

Pick one main source per course (the "spine") and treat everything else as a tool you reach for with a purpose. Sources can be lectures and slides, a textbook, the syllabus, an online course, YouTube, forums, past papers and question banks. Trying to cover every source fully is the most common way students drown.

**How to choose your spine.** The spine is the source closest to how you will be assessed or how you will use the knowledge. Ask: who writes the exam, and what do they teach from? If your lecturer writes the exam from their own slides, the slides are the spine, even if a famous textbook is better written. If the course follows a set textbook chapter by chapter (common in school, maths, physics and economics), the textbook is the spine and the lectures are a guided tour of it. If there is a public exam with a published specification (A levels, the bar exam, language certificates such as DELF or the Goethe exams, professional licensing exams), the specification and past papers define the spine, and you pick the one resource that maps most closely onto it. For a self-taught learner with no exam, the spine is one complete, well-structured course or book that you commit to finishing, for example one programming course rather than five half-watched ones.

**Examples from different fields.** A law student whose exams are problem questions uses the lecturer's course outline as the spine and reads cases only when the outline points to them. A first-year physics student uses the assigned textbook as the spine, because the problem sheets are taken from it, and uses lecture videos only for derivations she cannot follow. A language learner preparing for a B2 exam uses one coursebook series as the spine and past papers to check progress, with videos and podcasts as extra listening, not as new syllabus. A medical student uses lecture slides as the spine for school exams and a question bank as the spine for a national licensing exam. In each case the test is the same: if you only had time for one source, which one would leave you least surprised by the exam?

| Source | Role | How to use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lecture slides and lectures | Usually the spine | Exams are written by the lecturers, so their slides define what is tested. Annotate them and turn them into questions. |
| Learning objectives | The checklist | Every objective becomes a question you must answer with the book closed. |
| Coursebook or textbook | Reference | Look up only what the slides leave unclear. Read a chapter fully only when a topic is a real weakness. |
| YouTube and video platforms | First-pass understanding | Use for hard concepts (for example Khan Academy, Crash Course and 3Blue1Brown across many subjects; Ninja Nerd, Osmosis and Armando Hasudungan for medicine). Watch at 1.5x with a question in mind, then close it and recall. |
| Websites (for example Paul's Online Math Notes, MDN Web Docs for programming, the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy; Kenhub, TeachMeAnatomy, Radiopaedia and Geeky Medics for medicine) | Targeted look-ups | Quick clarification and images. Don't start reading new sites end to end. |
| Question banks and past papers | Retrieval and diagnosis | Use from early in the term, not only before exams. They show what you don't know. |
| Flashcard decks | Long-term retention | Make or unsuspend cards for what you just learned. |
| Classmates and students from earlier years | Calibration | Ask what past exams emphasised and which resources actually helped. |

### Rules that keep sources under control

#### One spine per course

Decide in week one. Every other source has to earn its place by fixing a gap.

**Why it works.** Every extra source adds a second voice that explains the same thing in different words, notation and order, and you spend effort translating between them instead of learning. A spine gives you one map, so new material always has a place to go. Research on choice overload is mixed (in a large meta-analysis the average effect was close to zero), but the effect appears when options are many, hard to compare and the chooser has no clear preference: exactly the position of a student facing ten resources for one course.

**How to do it.**

1. In week one, look at the syllabus, the assessment and past papers, and choose the source closest to them.
2. Write the spine at the top of your notes for the course.
3. Work through it in order. Leave it only to fix a specific gap, then come back.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A statistics student uses the course's own lecture notes and ignores three textbooks the reading list calls "optional", opening one only when a proof is unclear.
- *Non-trivial:* A self-taught programmer keeps switching between tutorials every time one gets hard. He picks one complete course as his spine and allows himself the official documentation (for example MDN Web Docs for web development) only to look things up. The hard parts are still hard, but now he gets through them.

> **📚 Further reading**
>
> - Article: [Choice Overload Bias](https://thedecisionlab.com/biases/choice-overload-bias), The Decision Lab
> - Paper: Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd (2010). [Can there ever be too many options? A meta-analytic review of choice overload](https://doi.org/10.1086/651235). *Journal of Consumer Research*.

#### Two questions before opening a new source

Will this be tested (or will I really use it), and does it fix a specific confusion? If neither, skip it.

**Why it works.** New sources are pleasant: a fresh voice, a nicer diagram, the feeling of starting over. That pleasure is exactly why they are dangerous, because they feel like study while adding little. Two quick questions turn an impulse into a decision. They also force you to name the confusion, which is half of solving it.

**How to do it.**

1. Before you open a video, book or website, say both questions out loud or write them down.
2. If the answer to the second is yes, write the confusion as a question ("Why does the integral of 1/x give ln x?").
3. Use the source until that question is answered, then close it and go back to the spine.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A biology student is about to watch a 40-minute video on cell signalling "to get a better overview". Neither answer is yes; she skips it and does the past-paper question instead.
- *Non-trivial:* A history student reads that a classmate swears by a new 600-page book. The topic will be tested, but she has no specific confusion, so she reads only the chapter on the one debate her notes handle badly. One chapter answers her question; the other 550 pages would have answered questions she did not have.

> **📚 Further reading**
>
> - Paper: Iyengar & Lepper (2000). [When choice is demotivating: Can one desire too much of a good thing?](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.995). *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*.

#### Consume less, retrieve more

An hour of video or reading followed by no recall is mostly lost.

**Why it works.** Watching a clear explanation feels like understanding, because you follow every step while it plays. But following is recognition, not recall. Without retrieval most of it fades within days, the same way a film's plot fades if you never talk about it. Short tests inside a lecture also keep your mind from wandering: in one experiment, students who were quizzed between segments of an online lecture wandered less, took more notes and remembered more.

**How to do it.**

1. Before you watch or read, write one question you want answered.
2. Stop every 10-15 minutes and recall the last part without looking.
3. At the end, close the source and write a short blank-page summary or answer three questions.
4. As a rule of thumb, match every hour of input with at least half an hour of recall or practice.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student watches a 12-minute Khan Academy video on supply and demand at 1.5x, pauses it twice to predict the next step, then draws the diagram from memory.
- *Non-trivial:* A guitar student watches a lesson on a new chord progression. Instead of watching it three times, she watches once, then plays it from memory, finds where she gets lost, and replays only that 30 seconds. Retrieval here is physical, but the principle is the same.

**⏱ Time cost.** Re-watching or re-reading costs \~1 min per paragraph (\~2×) for little gain; one recall question costs \~30 s (1×) and does more. Rough estimates.

> - Paper: Szpunar, Khan & Schacter (2013). [Interpolated memory tests reduce mind wandering and improve learning of online lectures](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1221764110). *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*.
> - Paper: Roediger & Karpicke (2006). [Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x). *Psychological Science*.

#### One home for notes

Keep all notes for a course in one place (one notebook, one Notion page, one folder). Link to external resources rather than copying them.

**Why it works.** Notes split across apps, notebooks and screenshots are notes you cannot review, because you never see them together. One home means one place to test yourself from. Linking rather than copying keeps notes short, and it matches how memory already works with external information: people remember where to find something even when they do not keep the thing itself in their head.

**How to do it.**

1. Choose one tool per course and stay with it for the term.
2. Organise it by topic, following the spine's order.
3. When a source helps, write the key point in your own words and add a link or page number, not a copy.
4. Collect loose notes (phone photos, sticky notes) into the home once a week.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A school student keeps one exercise book per subject and writes "see textbook p. 84, figure 3" instead of copying the figure.
- *Non-trivial:* A computer science student has notes in three apps. She moves everything into one folder per course, with one page per topic and links to the documentation, the lecture recording and the relevant past-paper question. Before the exam, revising a topic means opening one page, not searching three apps.

#### A source map per course

A short list: the spine, the reference book, the 2 or 3 go-to channels or sites, the question bank or past papers, and the flashcard deck.

**Why it works.** A source map is a decision you make once, while calm, so you do not have to make it again every time you are stuck and tired. It turns "where should I look?" into a reflex. Planning your resources in advance is one of the habits that separate self-regulated learners from students who react to whatever is in front of them.

**How to do it.**

1. In week one or two, write the map on the first page of your notes.
2. Keep it to five or six lines, with one role per source.
3. Review it once mid-term: drop what you never used, and add only to fill a real gap.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A maths student's map: lecture notes (spine), Paul's Online Math Notes (worked examples), 3Blue1Brown (intuition), last five years' exam papers (practice), own flashcards (definitions).
- *Non-trivial:* A philosophy student's map has no question bank, so she uses essay questions from past papers as retrieval and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy as her only reference. When a friend recommends a podcast, she checks the map: it does not fill any gap, so it stays off.

> **📚 Further reading**
>
> - Paper: Zimmerman (2002). [Becoming a self-regulated learner: An overview](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15430421tip4102_2). *Theory Into Practice*.

#### Avoid resource hoarding

Buying and bookmarking resources feels like progress, but it isn't. Finish one before adding another.

**Why it works.** Saving a resource gives you a small reward, the sense of having done something about the topic, without any learning. Christian Tietze calls this the collector's fallacy: knowing about a source is mistaken for knowing what is in it. A folder of 200 bookmarks also creates a quiet guilt that makes starting any of them harder.

**How to do it.**

1. Keep a single "later" list and do not act on it during term.
2. Before you add a resource, finish or delete one you already have.
3. Once a month, delete what you have not opened.
4. Judge your week by what you recalled and practised, not by what you collected.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A language learner has six apps installed. She keeps one for vocabulary and one for listening, and deletes the rest.
- *Non-trivial:* A medical student buys three question banks "to be safe" and finishes none, so his scores never tell him anything reliable. With one bank finished twice, he would know exactly which topics are weak. Depth in one resource gives you data; breadth across many gives you only samples.

## Planning and organisation across a term

The goal is to never fall more than a few days behind, because in any cumulative course a backlog grows faster than it can be cleared. A fixed weekly rhythm does most of the work. This holds for a school term, a university semester, a school year, or a self-study project such as a language or a professional certification.

Why backlogs are so dangerous: new material usually builds on old material. If you are three lessons behind in calculus, statistics or anatomy, today's lesson makes less sense, so it takes longer to learn, so you fall further behind. Debt in learning compounds like debt with interest. Planning is mostly about keeping that interest from ever starting.

### The lifecycle of one lesson, lecture or chapter

Every unit of new material (a lecture, a school lesson, a textbook chapter, a week of a language course, a module of an online course) goes through the same six steps. Most of the time cost sits in steps 3 to 5, and they are also where most of the learning happens.

1. **Before (10 to 15 min): skim or pretest.** Skim the headings, figures and learning objectives, or try two or three questions on the topic before you study it. *Why:* your brain listens better when it already has hooks to hang things on and questions it wants answered. Getting a pretest question wrong is useful: it primes you to notice the answer when it comes. *Example:* before a history lecture on the causes of the First World War, write down the three causes you would guess. During the lecture you will notice which ones you missed.
2. **During: attend actively.** Annotate the slides or your notes, and mark anything that confuses you with a "?" so you can find it later. *Why:* marking confusion in the moment costs a second and saves twenty minutes of hunting later. Passive listening feels like learning but leaves little behind. *Example:* in a programming class, write "? why does this loop stop at n-1" next to the code instead of trying to work it out while the teacher moves on.
3. **Same day (20 to 30 min): blank-page recall, then fill gaps.** Close everything, write or sketch what you remember, then open the source and fill the gaps in another colour. Clear up the "?" marks with the textbook or a short video. *Why:* forgetting is steepest in the first day. One effortful recall on the same day roughly resets the forgetting curve and tells you exactly what you did not understand while the lesson is still fresh. *Example:* after an economics lecture on elasticity, you can draw the demand curve but cannot explain why luxury goods are more elastic. That gap is your next 10 minutes.
4. **Within 24 hours: make or select flashcards.** Turn the facts that have no logic of their own (dates, vocabulary, formulas, names) into a few cards, or switch on the matching cards in a premade deck. *Why:* cards made while the material is fresh are better cards, and the spacing schedule starts on day one instead of the week before the exam. *Example:* after a Spanish lesson on the preterite, make 5 cloze cards for irregular forms ("ayer yo \_\_\_ (ir) al cine" → fui).
5. **Following days: spaced reviews.** Cards daily; a quick blank-page recall of the whole topic on about day 3, 7, 14 and 30. *Why:* each review comes just as the memory starts to fade, which is when recalling it does the most good. A review takes far less time than relearning from zero. *Example:* a 5-minute recall of last week's chemistry topic on the bus is worth more than an hour of rereading it the night before the test.
6. **Weekly: mixed practice questions.** Do questions that mix this week's topics with older ones. *Why:* mixing forces you to recognise which idea a problem needs, which is exactly what exams and real life ask. It also keeps old topics alive. *Example:* a physics set with two momentum problems, two energy problems and one from last month's kinematics, in random order.

**⏱ Time cost.** For one hour-long lecture or one textbook chapter, the extra work around it is roughly 60 to 90 minutes spread over a month: about 15 min before, 25 min the same day, 15 min of cards, and a few short reviews. That sounds like a lot, but it replaces most of the cramming you would otherwise do later. In terms of the scale used in this compendium, the daily backbone (recall questions, cards) costs \~0.5-1× per item, and the expensive extras are kept for the hard ideas.

### Semester map

At the start, list every course, exam date and assessment in one calendar. Count back from each exam.

**Why it works.** Most deadline crises are not surprises; they are things that were visible in week one but never put next to each other. Seeing all deadlines on one page shows the collisions in advance: three essays due in the same week, or an exam the day after a project deadline. Counting back turns a distant date into a start date.

**How to do it.**

1. Collect every syllabus, course page or exam timetable.
2. Put every exam, deadline, presentation and lab report in one calendar (paper wall planner or digital).
3. For each big item, count back and mark a "start by" date and a "first draft" or "first full mock" date.
4. Shade the crunch weeks so you can protect them (no extra shifts, no trips).

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* a secondary school student writes all end-of-term test dates on a wall calendar and sees that maths and biology fall on the same Monday, so biology revision starts a week earlier.
- *Non-trivial:* a part-time MBA student working full time maps the term and sees that the corporate finance case and a busy work quarter-end collide. She books two days of annual leave in that week, before anyone else books them.

**Watch out.** A map you make once and never look at again is decoration. Look at it during every weekly plan.

### Weekly plan

Once a week (for example Sunday, about 20 minutes), look at the week's lessons, due reviews and deadlines. Block study time in the calendar like appointments.

**Why it works.** A week is the right size: big enough to balance subjects, small enough to predict. People are much better at estimating time when they break a job into concrete parts (Kruger & Evans 2004), and a weekly plan is exactly that. It also moves decisions out of the moment: on Tuesday at 6 pm you do not have to decide what to study, only to follow the plan.

**How to do it.**

1. Look at the semester map: what is due in the next two weeks?
2. List the week's fixed events: classes, work, sport, family.
3. Put reviews in first (they are not optional), then new material, then assignments.
4. Leave white space; plan about 70 to 80% of your free time, not 100%.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* a first-year law student blocks Monday and Wednesday evenings for contract law reading and Thursday morning for a practice problem.
- *Non-trivial:* a nurse studying for a certification on rotating shifts plans her study blocks only after the roster is out, and puts the hardest topic on the day after a day off, not after a night shift.

**⏱ Time cost.** About 20 minutes a week. It usually pays for itself on the first day.

### Daily plan

Spend about 5 minutes picking 1 to 3 must-do tasks for the day. Everything else is a bonus.

**Why it works.** A long to-do list mixes the important with the trivial, and the trivial items are easier, so they win. Choosing a small number of must-dos makes success possible every day, which matters for motivation. Writing down a specific plan also quiets the mental nagging of unfinished goals (Masicampo & Baumeister 2011), so you can focus on the task in front of you.

**How to do it.**

1. Look at today's calendar block and the weekly plan.
2. Write 1 to 3 tasks that would make the day a success. Make them concrete ("blank-page recall of lecture 6", not "study biochem").
3. Do the first one before email, messages or anything else, if you can.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* "1. Anki reviews. 2. Recall today's history lesson. 3. Finish question 4 of the problem sheet."
- *Non-trivial:* a developer preparing for a cloud certification on a heavy work day writes only one must-do ("20 practice questions on networking at lunch") and treats anything more as a bonus. A small win on a bad day keeps the streak alive.

> **📚 Further reading**
>
> - Paper: Masicampo & Baumeister (2011). [Consider it done! Plan making can eliminate the cognitive effects of unfulfilled goals](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024192). *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*.

### Time blocking

Fixed study blocks at the same times each day turn studying into a routine rather than a daily decision.

**Why it works.** Every decision ("should I study now? what first?") costs willpower and gives procrastination an opening. A block on the calendar is a decision made in advance. Cal Newport, who popularised the term, describes giving every hour of the work day a job and then adjusting the plan when reality changes, rather than abandoning it.

**How to do it.**

1. Find 2 to 4 times in your week that are reliably free and when you have energy.
2. Give each block a label with a task ("19:00-20:30 Statistics: problem set 3").
3. If something overruns, move the block; do not delete it.
4. Use the same place and the same start ritual (water, phone in another room, timer) so starting becomes automatic.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* a high school student does 30 minutes of French vocabulary every day straight after dinner.
- *Non-trivial:* a music student blocks technical practice for the morning when her hands are fresh and theory homework for the evening, because the two use different kinds of energy. Matching the block to the energy matters as much as the time.

**Watch out.** Blocks that are too long or too packed get broken and then abandoned. 60 to 90 minutes with a break is a good default.

>
> - Article: [Deep Habits: The Importance of Planning Every Minute of Your Work Day](https://calnewport.com/deep-habits-the-importance-of-planning-every-minute-of-your-work-day/), Cal Newport

### Plan reviews before new material

Reviews first each day, then new content. Skipping reviews is how backlogs start.

**Why it works.** Reviews are small, due on a specific day, and get more expensive the longer you wait: a card reviewed on time takes 15 seconds, the same card forgotten takes several minutes to relearn. New material, by contrast, can wait a day without getting worse. If you do new material first, the reviews get squeezed out, then pile up, then feel overwhelming, and that is the compounding debt. Spaced reviews also give much better long-term retention for the same total time (Cepeda et al. 2006).

**How to do it.**

1. Start each study block with whatever reviews are due (cards, topic recall).
2. Only then move to new lessons or assignments.
3. If you are short on time, shrink new material, not reviews.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* a language learner does her 15 minutes of Anki before opening the next textbook unit.
- *Non-trivial:* an engineering student with 300 overdue cards after a holiday does not start new decks at all for three days. He works the overdue pile in 100-card batches, oldest topics first, and only then resumes new material. The backlog clears in under a week instead of growing.

**⏱ Time cost.** Reviews run at \~0.5-1× per item on the scale, the cheapest work in the whole plan.

> - Article: [How To Remember Anything Forever-ish](https://ncase.me/remember/), Nicky Case
> - Paper: Cepeda et al. (2006). [Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: A review and quantitative synthesis](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.3.354). *Psychological Bulletin*.

### A catch-up rule

If you fall behind, do not try to do everything. Do today's lessons first, then work the backlog in small chunks, highest-yield first.

**Why it works.** When you fall behind, the natural instinct is to go back to the oldest missed lecture and work forward. That keeps you permanently behind: you are always learning last week's material while this week's piles up. Staying current stops the debt growing; then you pay it off in instalments.

**How to do it.**

1. Keep up with today's and this week's material as if nothing were missing.
2. List the backlog and rank it: what is most likely to be tested, and what do upcoming topics depend on?
3. Add one or two small backlog chunks per day (one lecture, one chapter section).
4. For low-yield items, a quick skim plus a few recall questions may be enough.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* after a week off sick, a school student follows this week's maths lessons and catches up one missed lesson per evening.
- *Non-trivial:* a medical student two weeks behind in pharmacology ranks the missed lectures by how often their drug classes appear in past papers. She fully catches up on the top four and covers the rest with flashcards only, accepting that a perfect catch-up is not possible.

### Buffer days

Leave one lighter day per week for catch-up and rest.

**Why it works.** People consistently underestimate how long tasks will take, even when they know similar tasks overran before. This is the planning fallacy (Buehler, Griffin & Ross 1994). A plan with no slack breaks the first time something takes longer than expected, and a broken plan is often abandoned completely. A buffer absorbs the overrun so the rest of the week survives.

**How to do it.**

1. Mark one day (or two half-days) a week as "buffer": nothing new is scheduled.
2. Use it first for anything that slipped, then for rest.
3. If nothing slipped, the buffer is a reward, not a chance to cram more in.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Friday afternoon is the buffer: finish what slipped, then go out.
- *Non-trivial:* a doctoral student writing a thesis chapter plans her estimates, then multiplies them by 1.5. Over three months her real times matched the inflated estimates far better than her original guesses, and she stopped missing supervisor deadlines.

> - Paper: Buehler, Griffin & Ross (1994). [Exploring the "planning fallacy": Why people underestimate their task completion times](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366). *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*.

### Single capture system

Use one place for every task and deadline (a planner, a to-do app or a paper notebook). Nothing stays only in your head.

**Why it works.** An open loop (an unwritten task) keeps tugging at your attention, and it shows up at the worst moment, during study. David Allen's Getting Things Done method starts with "capture": collect everything that has your attention into one trusted inbox. Once your brain trusts the system to remember, it lets go. Having one place matters more than which app you use; two places means you trust neither.

**How to do it.**

1. Choose one tool and carry it always (phone app, pocket notebook).
2. Write down every task, deadline and idea the moment it appears.
3. Empty the inbox into your calendar or task list during the weekly plan.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* a student writes "essay topic due Fri" in the notes app the moment the teacher says it, instead of "remembering" it.
- *Non-trivial:* a self-taught programmer keeps a single text file with three headings: "to do", "to learn", "questions". Anything that would derail a focus session ("look up how Git rebase works") goes in it and gets sorted on Sunday.

> **📚 Further reading**

> - Article: [What is GTD?](https://gettingthingsdone.com/what-is-gtd/) (capture, clarify, organise, reflect, engage), Getting Things Done
> - Paper: Masicampo & Baumeister (2011). [Consider it done! Plan making can eliminate the cognitive effects of unfulfilled goals](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024192). *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*.

### Weekly review

Spend about 15 minutes on what got done, what slipped and what to change. This is where you adjust the method.

**Why it works.** Without feedback, a plan cannot improve; you just repeat the same mistakes with more guilt. The weekly review closes the loop: you compare your estimates with what happened (the direct cure for the planning fallacy), clear the capture inbox, and decide one change. David Allen calls the weekly review the critical success factor of his whole system.

**How to do it.** Answer three questions in writing:

1. What did I get done? (Done list, wins.)
2. What slipped, and why? (Too ambitious, bad timing, distraction, illness?)
3. What is one change for next week?

Then make next week's plan straight away, while the lessons are fresh.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* "Got done: all reviews, 4 of 5 lectures. Slipped: essay outline, because Thursday evenings are always taken. Change: move essay block to Saturday morning."
- *Non-trivial:* a language learner sees that her vocabulary scores rise but her speaking does not. She changes one block a week from cards to a 30-minute online conversation lesson, a change she would not have made without looking at the week as a whole.

## Focus, procrastination and ADHD-friendly strategies

With ADHD the problem is usually starting, switching and sustaining effort, not ability. The fix is to make starting tiny, keep everything visible, and build in variety and outside structure. Most of these tactics help anyone who procrastinates.

Procrastination is rarely laziness. Research by Fuschia Sirois and Tim Pychyl frames it as short-term mood repair: a task makes you feel bored, anxious or overwhelmed, and putting it off makes that feeling go away for now, at the cost of your future self. That is why "just try harder" fails, and why the strategies below work on the feeling and the first step instead. ADHD adds real differences in attention, working memory and time sense, so outside structure does more of the work. A note on evidence: some items below rest on solid research (implementation intentions, breaks, exercise and attention), others are mainly practitioner and community experience (body doubling, most ADHD study hacks). Each item says which. None of this replaces an assessment if you think you might have ADHD.

### Starting

#### The 5-minute rule

**Commit to only 5 minutes. Stopping after that is allowed. Most of the time you keep going.**

**Why it works.** Most of the dread sits *before* the task, not inside it, like the shock of a cold swimming pool that fades once you are in. Five minutes is too small for your mood-repair system to object to. Once you have started, an unfinished task tends to keep pulling at your attention (a Zeigarnik-like pull), so continuing feels easier than stopping. The original Zeigarnik memory effect replicates inconsistently, so treat that part as intuition rather than law.

**How to do it.**

1. Set a timer for 5 minutes and pick the smallest visible piece of the task.
2. Decide in advance that stopping at the bell is genuinely fine. The permission is what makes it work.
3. When the timer rings, choose on purpose: stop, or set another block.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A language learner who has skipped a week does 5 minutes of vocabulary on the bus and ends up doing fifteen.
- *Non-trivial:* A law student dreading a 3,000-word essay commits to 5 minutes of writing a deliberately bad first paragraph. The bar is "bad", so perfectionism has nothing to grip. Rewriting a bad paragraph is far easier than writing a good one from nothing.

**Watch out.** If you stop at five minutes every single time, the task is too big or too vague: use the next item.

>
> - Article: [Procrastination Problem? Tim Pychyl Knows Why](https://carleton.ca/news/story/procrastination-problem-tim-pychyl/), Carleton University News
> - Paper: Sirois, F. & Pychyl, T. (2013). [Procrastination and the priority of short-term mood regulation: Consequences for future self](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12011). *Social and Personality Psychology Compass*.

#### Make the next step absurdly small

**Not "study chemistry" but "open lecture 4 and read the first slide".**

**Why it works.** "Study chemistry" is a project, not an action: your brain cannot see where to put its hands, so it looks for something easier. A step that is physical, specific and under two minutes leaves no room for the "where do I even start" feeling that triggers avoidance. It is the same reason a recipe says "chop one onion", not "make dinner".

**How to do it.**

1. Write the next step as verb + object + place: "open the essay prompt in the course folder".
2. If you still hesitate, halve it again until it feels almost silly.
3. After each step, write the next one before you stop, so tomorrow starts with a ready-made action.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A history student replaces "revise the French Revolution" with "find the timeline handout and read the first five dates".
- *Non-trivial:* A programmer stuck on a vague bug replaces "fix the login bug" with "write one test that reproduces it". The small step is also the *diagnostic* step, so it shrinks the task and clarifies it at the same time. A medical student does the same with "open the cardiology deck and do 10 cards".

> **📚 Further reading**
>
> - Article: [How Small Wins Unleash Creativity](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/how-small-wins-unleash-creativity), Harvard Business School Working Knowledge

#### Implementation intentions

**Decide in advance "when X happens, I will do Y" ("after lunch, I sit at my desk and do my flashcards").**

**Why it works.** A goal ("study more") still needs a decision every day about when and where, and each decision is a chance to postpone. An if-then plan hands the decision to the situation: the cue ("after lunch") triggers the action almost automatically. A large meta-analysis by Gollwitzer and Sheeran found a medium-to-large effect on actually reaching goals, one of the best-supported tools in this section.

**How to do it.**

1. Pick a reliable cue that already happens: a time, a place or the end of another habit.
2. Write: "When \[cue\], I will \[small, specific action\] \[where\]."
3. Add one obstacle plan: "If \[likely obstacle\], then I will \[response\]."

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* "When I get home from school, I put my bag down and do 20 flashcards at the kitchen table."
- *Non-trivial:* A physics student plans for the obstacle rather than the start: "If I am stuck on a problem for more than 10 minutes, I will write down exactly where I am stuck and move to the next one." This stops one hard problem from eating the whole evening and turns being stuck into a note for the tutor.

**Watch out.** Vague cues ("in the afternoon") work poorly. So do plans that stack too many if-thens to remember.

> - Paper: Gollwitzer, P. M. & Sheeran, P. (2006). [Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>). *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*.

#### Reduce friction

**Leave the desk ready, the book open, the app on the right page. Make distractions harder: phone in another room, site blockers (Cold Turkey, Freedom, Forest).**

**Why it works.** Every extra step between you and the task is an exit; every extra step between you and a distraction is a speed bump. Willpower is unreliable, so change the slope of the floor instead. Ward and colleagues (2017) found that people with their phone in another room did better on working-memory tasks than people with the phone face down on the desk, as if part of the mind was busy not checking it. A later replication did not find the effect, so treat it as plausible rather than proven. The practical advice holds either way: out of reach is out of the loop.

**How to do it.**

1. At the end of each session, set up the start of the next one (book open, file open, pen on the page).
2. Put the phone in another room or a drawer during focus blocks; use a cheap kitchen timer so the phone is not your clock.
3. Turn on a blocker for your two worst sites only during planned work hours.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A music student leaves the guitar on a stand instead of in its case, and practises far more often.
- *Non-trivial:* An economics student blocks news and video sites from 9 to 12 but not in the evening. Blocking everything all the time leads to switching the blocker off; a narrow, timed block is one you keep.

> - Paper: Ward, A. F., Duke, K., Gneezy, A. & Bos, M. W. (2017). [Brain drain: The mere presence of one's own smartphone reduces available cognitive capacity](https://doi.org/10.1086/691462). *Journal of the Association for Consumer Research*.

### Sustaining focus

#### Pomodoro and variants

**25 minutes of work and 5 of rest is the classic. Some people do better with 15/5 or 50/10. A visible timer helps.**

**Why it works.** A fixed end point makes effort feel finite, which calms the "this will never end" feeling. Planned breaks come before fatigue rather than after it. In a study of university students, Biwer and colleagues (2023) found that fixed breaks (Pomodoro style) led to better mood and the same work done in less time than breaks taken whenever students felt like it. A meta-analysis of short breaks (Albulescu et al., 2022) found more energy and less fatigue after breaks of 10 minutes or less, but no clear overall gain in performance, so treat breaks as fuel for the next block rather than a shortcut.

**How to do it.**

1. Choose one task for the block and start a visible timer.
2. When the timer rings, stand up and leave the screen for the break.
3. After four blocks, take a longer break (15-30 minutes).
4. Adjust the lengths to your attention span; the rhythm matters more than the numbers.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A school student does maths homework in 25/5 blocks, two problems per block.
- *Non-trivial:* A medical student who hyperfocuses uses the bell differently: not to start, but to *stop* and drink water, so a four-hour session without a break does not wreck the evening. A novelist in full flow may stretch to 50/10 instead, trading the classic rhythm for fewer interruptions.

**Watch out.** Breaks on the phone often stretch from 5 minutes to 30. Make the break physical.

> - Paper: Biwer, F., Wiradhany, W., oude Egbrink, M. & de Bruin, A. (2023). [Understanding effort regulation: Comparing 'Pomodoro' breaks and self-regulated breaks](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjep.12593). *British Journal of Educational Psychology*.

#### Body doubling

**Study next to someone else who is working, in person, in a library, or online (Focusmate, study-with-me videos).**

**Why it works.** Probable reasons: a mild sense of being seen, a clear signal that "this is work time", and less of the isolation that makes avoidance easy. The other person does not help with the task; they only need to be there, working. Be honest about the evidence: body doubling is widely recommended by ADHD coaches and communities, but it is mostly anecdotal and practitioner-based. A 2025 research paper that reviewed the studies so far concluded that none has clearly shown it works. It is cheap and low-risk, so it is worth testing on yourself.

**How to do it.**

1. Pick a setting: a friend at the table, a library, or an online session.
2. Say your goal for the session out loud or in the chat at the start ("draft section 2").
3. At the end, say briefly what you got done.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Two friends revise different subjects at the same library table and agree not to talk until the break.
- *Non-trivial:* An accountancy student uses 50-minute Focusmate sessions with a stranger. Naming a concrete goal to someone who will ask about it at the end adds accountability that a study-with-me video, which never checks, cannot.

> **📚 Further reading**
>

> - Tool: [Focusmate](https://www.focusmate.com/), online co-working sessions
> - Paper: Ara, Z. et al. (2025). [You Are Not Alone: Designing Body Doubling for ADHD in Virtual Reality](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.12153v1). *arXiv preprint* (includes a review of the limited evidence).

#### Distraction parking lot

**Keep a sheet next to you. When a thought pops up ("reply to that message", "look up X"), write it down and return to the task.**

**Why it works.** Unfinished intentions keep knocking on the door of your attention until they feel handled. Masicampo and Baumeister (2011) found that making a concrete plan for an unfinished goal reduced its intrusions on a later task. Writing the thought down is a tiny version of that: your mind trusts the sheet. It also stops a switch. Leroy (2009) showed that when you jump to another task and back, part of your attention stays behind ("attention residue"), so a 20-second note costs far less than a 2-minute detour.

**How to do it.**

1. Keep paper (not your phone) next to you.
2. Write the thought in enough words to act on later, then go straight back.
3. At the break or the end of the session, process the list: do it, schedule it or cross it out.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* "Buy a birthday card" goes on the sheet instead of into a shopping app.
- *Non-trivial:* A data science student mid-analysis thinks "check whether the dataset has duplicate IDs". It is relevant, which makes it more tempting. Parking it with context ("duplicates? check ID column after the plot") keeps the current line of thought intact.

> **📚 Further reading**
>
> - Paper: Masicampo, E. J. & Baumeister, R. F. (2011). [Consider it done! Plan making can eliminate the cognitive effects of unfulfilled goals](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024192). *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*.
> - Paper: Leroy, S. (2009). [Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v109y2009i2p168-181.html). *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*.

#### Movement

**Walk while doing flashcards or explaining a topic aloud. Use a standing desk. Take active breaks.**

**Why it works.** Sitting still can be effortful in itself for many people with ADHD, and that effort competes with the material. Gentle movement can keep alertness up. There is also good evidence that bouts of aerobic exercise improve attention and executive function in the short term, and regular fitness supports them over the long term (Hillman et al., 2008). Evidence that moving *while* studying improves learning for students with ADHD is limited, so test it on yourself.

**How to do it.**

1. Pair movement with low-writing tasks: flashcards, recall aloud, listening.
2. Use active breaks: a short brisk walk or stairs, not scrolling.
3. Keep demanding writing and problem-solving for seated blocks.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A language learner walks around the block doing vocabulary cards on paper.
- *Non-trivial:* A medical student paces the room explaining the cardiac cycle aloud without notes. The movement keeps energy up, and saying it aloud exposes gaps that silent reading hides.

> **📚 Further reading**
>
> - Paper: Hillman, C. H., Erickson, K. I. & Kramer, A. F. (2008). [Be smart, exercise your heart: exercise effects on brain and cognition](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2298). *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*.

#### Variety inside structure

**Rotate methods within a session (cards, then drawing, then questions) so novelty keeps attention up while the plan stays fixed.**

**Why it works.** Attention fades with sameness, and a fresh format gives it a small restart. The trick is to vary the *method* while keeping the *topic and plan* fixed. Hopping between topics or apps every few minutes is a different thing: Ophir and colleagues (2009) found that heavy media multitaskers were worse at filtering out distractions (a correlational finding), and each switch leaves attention residue.

**How to do it.**

1. Plan one topic for the session and two or three methods (for example 20 minutes cards, 20 minutes sketching, 20 minutes practice questions).
2. Switch method at the timer, not when you feel bored.
3. Keep the order the same each day so the variety does not cost decisions.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A biology student does cards, then labels a blank diagram, then answers three exam questions, all on the kidney.
- *Non-trivial:* A Spanish learner works on one theme (travel) through listening, then speaking aloud, then writing a short message. The variety keeps attention up, and meeting the same words in three formats is also stronger learning than three rounds of one format.

> **📚 Further reading**
>
> - Paper: Ophir, E., Nass, C. & Wagner, A. D. (2009). [Cognitive control in media multitaskers](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0903620106). *PNAS*.

#### Interest hooks

**Start each topic with a case, a puzzle or a "why" question that makes you curious.**

**Why it works.** Curiosity changes how well you remember. In Gruber and colleagues' study (2014), when people were curious about a question, they remembered the answer better, and even remembered unrelated faces shown in that state. A popular framing in the ADHD world, Dr William Dodson's "interest-based nervous system", says ADHD attention runs on interest, novelty, challenge and urgency rather than importance. It is a clinician's description, not an established scientific model, but many people find it a useful way to understand themselves.

**How to do it.**

1. Before a topic, write one question you genuinely want answered.
2. Use a real case, a surprising fact or a problem you cannot yet solve.
3. Read or study to answer that question first.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A history student starts with "How did an empire this large collapse in fifty years?"
- *Non-trivial:* A medical student opens the topic with a clinical case (a young athlete who collapses on the pitch) before the theory of heart rhythm. A physics student starts with "why does a spinning top not fall over?" The hook gives every fact that follows a place to hang.

>
> - Article: [3 Defining Features of ADHD That Everyone Overlooks](https://www.additudemag.com/symptoms-of-add-hyperarousal-rejection-sensitivity/), ADDitude (William Dodson, MD)
> - Paper: Gruber, M. J., Gelman, B. D. & Ranganath, C. (2014). [States of curiosity modulate hippocampus-dependent learning via the dopaminergic circuit](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2014.08.060). *Neuron*.

### Staying organised

#### Externalise everything

**Calendars, alarms, checklists and visible lists replace working memory.**

**Why it works.** Working memory is a small whiteboard, and with ADHD it is often smaller or gets wiped more easily. Everything you are "trying to remember" takes up space you need for the actual work. Moving it onto paper or a screen frees that space, and the reminder arrives at the right moment instead of at 3 a.m.

**How to do it.**

1. Every deadline goes into one calendar the moment you hear it.
2. Set alarms for the start of tasks, not only for deadlines.
3. Use checklists for repeating routines (packing for an exam, submitting an assignment).

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A school student sets a phone alarm labelled "PE kit" for the evening before sports day.
- *Non-trivial:* A nursing student builds a checklist for every placement shift (badge, notebook, questions to ask). Making it once costs ten minutes; it saves the same mental scan before every single shift.

#### Visible progress

**A wall planner, a progress bar, ticked boxes. Seeing the work done is motivating.**

**Why it works.** Amabile and Kramer's research on work diaries ("the progress principle") found that making progress on meaningful work, even small steps, was the strongest everyday booster of motivation. Long study projects hide progress: chapter 3 looks the same whether you understood it or not. Making progress visible gives you the small wins your effort earned.

**How to do it.**

1. Break the course into visible units (lectures, chapters, problem sets).
2. Put them on paper where you can see them, and tick or colour each one when done.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student colours one square per finished lecture on a printed grid above the desk.
- *Non-trivial:* A self-taught programmer tracks finished exercises in a public repository. The visible streak motivates, but they count *exercises solved*, not hours logged, so the bar rewards progress rather than sitting time.

#### Done list

**Write down what you did, not just what is left.**

**Why it works.** A to-do list never ends, so it can make a productive day feel like failure. A done list shows evidence of effort, which counters the "I did nothing today" story and the guilt that feeds the next round of avoidance. It also gives you data for your weekly review.

**How to do it.** At the end of each block, write one line: what you did. At the end of the week, read it back.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* "Read pages 40-52, did 30 cards" in a notebook.
- *Non-trivial:* A PhD student in law keeps a done list during a slow research phase. On bad days it shows that "nothing" was actually four cases read and two dead ends ruled out, which is real progress in research.

#### Rewards right after

**A small, immediate reward after each block works better than a distant reward.**

**Why it works.** People value rewards less the further away they are, and a good grade in June is very far from a Tuesday evening. Many people with ADHD find delays especially hard to wait through. A small reward right after the block brings some of the payoff into the present.

**How to do it.**

1. Choose small rewards that do not swallow the next block: a coffee, a short walk, one song.
2. Link the reward to finishing the block, not to the mood.
3. Save bigger rewards for bigger milestones.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* After three Pomodoros, a favourite snack.
- *Non-trivial:* A music student "bundles" a reward with the task: the good coffee is only for scale practice. The reward is attached to doing the boring part, not to getting through it.

**Watch out.** Rewards that open endless content (one episode, one video) often turn into the whole evening.

#### Time awareness

**Use timers and analogue clocks; ADHD often comes with time blindness.**

**Why it works.** "Time blindness" is a popular term for a real pattern: many people with ADHD have trouble sensing how much time has passed and estimating how long tasks will take, so it is "now" or "not now". An analogue clock or a visual countdown timer shows time as a shrinking space you can see, rather than a number you have to interpret.

**How to do it.**

1. Put an analogue clock or a visual timer in sight while you work.
2. Before a task, guess how long it will take; afterwards, write down the real time. Over a few weeks your estimates improve.
3. Set leave-the-house alarms that count back from the start time, including travel.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student puts a visual timer on the desk during homework.
- *Non-trivial:* An engineering student logs estimated versus actual time for each problem set and finds they take 1.8 times longer than planned. They now plan with that multiplier, which fixes their schedule more than any extra effort did.

### Support

#### Get assessed and supported

**If ADHD is suspected or diagnosed, a doctor can discuss treatment, and school or university disability services, or your employer, can arrange accommodations such as extra exam time or a quiet room.**

**Why it works.** Study tactics help, but they work on top of the underlying condition, not instead of treating it. For ADHD, medication is among the best-studied treatments in psychiatry: a large network meta-analysis (Cortese et al., 2018) found that stimulant medication reduced core symptoms in children and adults in the short term. Medication is not right for everyone, and therapy, coaching and skills training are options too; that is a conversation for a doctor. Accommodations remove obstacles that strategy alone cannot, such as noisy exam halls.

**How to do it.**

1. Book an appointment with a doctor and say what you notice (starting, focus, time, organisation) and since when.
2. Contact your school's or university's disability or accessibility service early; assessments and paperwork can take weeks.
3. At work, ask HR or your manager about reasonable accommodations (a quieter workspace, written instructions, flexible deadlines where possible).

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A university student registers with the accessibility service and gets exams in a smaller room with extra time.
- *Non-trivial:* A junior doctor, diagnosed as an adult, combines treatment with simple workplace adjustments: written handover checklists and a quiet spot for documentation. A small change in setting often helps more than another productivity app.

#### Coaching and peers

**ADHD coaches, study groups and accountability partners help with structure.**

**Why it works.** Structure is easier to keep when someone else holds part of it. A coach helps you set goals, plan and review; a peer or study group adds regular check-ins. In one study of 148 college students, eight weeks of ADHD coaching was followed by better study skills, self-esteem and satisfaction with school. The study had no control group, so the evidence is promising rather than conclusive.

**How to do it.**

1. Find one accountability partner and agree on a weekly 10-minute check-in: plan, results, next step.
2. Join or form a study group with fixed times.
3. If you can, look for an ADHD coach through your university or a reputable professional body.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Two classmates text each other their three goals every Monday and their results every Friday.
- *Non-trivial:* An adult learner retraining in accounting meets a coach fortnightly. Instead of discussing motivation, they review last fortnight's plan against the done list and adjust one thing. The coach's value is in that steady review loop, which is hard to keep up alone.

## Anxiety, stress and wellbeing

Regular self-testing lowers exam anxiety, because it replaces vague worry with concrete evidence of what you know. Alongside that, a few habits keep stress at a level that helps rather than blocks.

Some stress is useful: it sharpens attention and gets you to the desk. The trouble starts when worry takes up the mental space you need for the work itself. Working memory, the small "desk" your mind thinks on, is shared between the task and the worry, so a head full of "what if I fail" has less room for the equation in front of it. The items below either shrink the worry, free up that desk, or keep your body (sleep, movement, food) in a state where learning can happen. A note on limits: these are self-help habits for everyday study stress, not treatment. If anxiety, low mood or exhaustion is lasting for weeks, affecting sleep, eating or daily life, or you have thoughts of harming yourself, please talk to a professional (see the last item).

### Test yourself often, in low-stakes ways

**Frequent practice makes the real exam feel familiar.**

**Why it works.** Anxiety feeds on uncertainty. When you only re-read, you never find out whether you could answer under pressure, so the exam is a leap into the dark. Low-stakes quizzing turns the unknown into a known: you have already seen yourself retrieve the material many times. It is like a rehearsal before a concert; the stage is still scary, but it is a stage you have stood on. In a large classroom study, most middle and high school students said regular retrieval practice made them less nervous about tests.

**How to do it.**

1. Quiz yourself in every session with questions where nothing is at stake: flashcards, blank-page recall, a few past questions.
2. Treat wrong answers as information ("I need to revisit X"), never as a verdict.
3. In the last weeks, add one or two full practice papers under timed, exam-like conditions.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A school student does five quick questions on each chemistry topic at the start of each lesson-review, so the test feels like "just more of the same".
- *Non-trivial:* A law student who freezes in oral exams asks a friend to quiz them out loud once a week. The content is the same as the written practice, but rehearsing the *format* (speaking, being watched) is what lowers the fear.

**Watch out.** If every practice set feels like a judgement on your worth, lower the stakes further: do it alone, ungraded, or with an open book for the first round.

### Worry dump

**Spend 5 to 10 minutes writing down your worries before studying or before an exam. It frees up working memory.**

**Why it works.** Worries behave like browser tabs left open: each one quietly uses some of your mental capacity, even when you are not looking at it. Writing them down closes some of the tabs. Once a worry is on paper, your mind no longer has to keep rehearsing it in case it gets forgotten. In a study published in *Science*, students who wrote freely about their test worries for 10 minutes before an exam did better than those who did not, and the benefit was largest for the most anxious students.

**How to do it.**

1. Before a session or exam, set a timer for 5 to 10 minutes.
2. Write continuously about what you are worried about and how you feel. Spelling and logic do not matter; nobody will read it.
3. Close the page (or throw it away) and start the first task straight away.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Before a maths test, a student writes "I always mess up fractions, I'm worried about running out of time" and then starts the paper feeling lighter.
- *Non-trivial:* A doctoral student with a looming viva keeps a running "worry file". Each morning she adds to it for five minutes, then adds one concrete action next to each worry where an action exists ("ask supervisor about chapter 3 methods"). The writing empties the head; the action column stops the same worry returning tomorrow.

**⏱ Time cost.** 5 to 10 minutes per session, not per paragraph. Usually repaid by a calmer, more focused start.

### Define a stopping point

**Anxious students often never feel "done". Decide in advance what a finished day looks like, and stop when you reach it.**

**Why it works.** There is always more you could study, so "until I feel ready" never arrives. Without a finish line, the day ends with guilt instead of rest, and that guilt follows you into the evening. A pre-set target gives you a way to win the day. Research on unfinished goals also shows that making a concrete plan for what remains is enough to stop those goals intruding on your thoughts: the mind lets go once it trusts there is a plan.

**How to do it.**

1. In the morning (or the night before), write the day's finish line: "3 practice question sets, review cards, outline essay section 2".
2. When it is done, write tomorrow's first task on a note. This is your "shutdown".
3. Close the books and actually stop, even if you have energy left. Spare energy is tomorrow's head start.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A language learner's finished day is "today's flashcards plus one 15-minute conversation". After that, the evening is free.
- *Non-trivial:* An engineering student in exam season sets a finish line by *output*, not hours ("two past papers marked and errors logged"). On a good day she is done by 4 pm and stops anyway; on a bad day she accepts finishing late rather than moving the line. The point is that the line is decided by the calm morning self, not the anxious evening self.

### Productive struggle vs panic

**Struggling to recall is normal and useful. Panic is a signal to pause, breathe and shrink the task.**

**Why it works.** The effort of pulling an answer out of memory is exactly what strengthens it (a "desirable difficulty"), like the strain of a muscle during a workout. But there is a line: once stress floods your working memory, you stop thinking and start spinning. The skill is telling the two apart. Struggle feels like "it is on the tip of my tongue"; panic feels like "I can't do any of this".

**How to do it.**

1. When you get stuck, give yourself a short, fixed attempt (for example 1 to 2 minutes), then check the answer. That is productive struggle.
2. If you notice racing thoughts, a tight chest or the urge to flee, stop. Do one minute of slow breathing (next item).
3. Shrink the task until it feels doable again: one question instead of the whole set, one paragraph instead of the chapter.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student blanks on a vocabulary word, gives it 30 seconds, checks, and moves on. That is struggle working as intended.
- *Non-trivial:* A physics student facing a long, unfamiliar problem feels panic rising. Instead of staring at the whole thing, she writes down only what is given and what is asked. That small, certain step calms her enough to see which equation fits. Panic shrank because the task did.

### Breathing

**A slow exhale, or two inhales through the nose and one long exhale (the physiological sigh), calms the body within a minute.**

**Why it works.** Breathing is one of the few parts of the stress response you can control directly. Long, slow exhales nudge the nervous system towards its "rest" mode and slow the heart; the double inhale reopens the tiny air sacs in the lungs so the long exhale clears more carbon dioxide. Think of it as a manual brake for a body that has hit the accelerator. In a month-long randomised study, five minutes a day of "cyclic sighing" improved mood more than mindfulness meditation and lowered breathing rate.

**How to do it.**

1. Inhale through your nose until your lungs feel comfortably full.
2. Take a second, shorter inhale on top to fill them completely.
3. Breathe out slowly through your mouth until empty. Make the exhale longer than the inhales.
4. Repeat 1 to 3 times for a quick reset, or for about 5 minutes as a daily practice.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Two physiological sighs in the waiting room before an oral exam or a driving test.
- *Non-trivial:* A music student uses five minutes of cyclic sighing every morning during audition season, not just before performing. The daily practice makes the technique automatic, so it is available on the day instead of being one more new thing to remember under stress.

**⏱ Time cost.** Under a minute for a reset; 5 minutes for a daily practice.

**Watch out.** If you feel dizzy, slow down and breathe normally. Breathing exercises help with everyday stress but are not a substitute for treatment of an anxiety disorder.

### Reframe stress

**Telling yourself that a racing heart is your body getting ready to perform helps more than trying to calm down.**

**Why it works.** Anxiety and excitement feel almost the same in the body: fast heart, sweaty hands, alertness. What differs is the label. "I'm anxious, this is bad" turns the arousal into a threat signal; "my body is gearing up" turns it into fuel. Trying to force yourself calm often fails, because you are fighting a physical state. Relabelling works with it instead. In one study, students told before a practice exam that arousal can improve performance scored higher on the maths section, both in the lab and on the real exam later.

**How to do it.**

1. Notice the signs (heart, breath, stomach).
2. Say to yourself, silently or on paper: "This is my body getting ready. Energy, not danger."
3. Point the energy at the first concrete action: read question 1.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* An athlete's pre-race nerves are the same as a student's pre-exam nerves; athletes simply learn to call them "readiness".
- *Non-trivial:* A trainee lawyer before her first moot court feels her heart pounding. Instead of "calm down", she thinks "good, I'm switched on". She still feels the arousal, but it stops snowballing into "something is wrong with me", and she uses the alertness to listen sharply to the judge's questions.

**Watch out.** Reframing works for normal performance stress. It is not meant to override signals that something is genuinely wrong, such as panic attacks or anxiety that lasts all day.

### Self-compassion over perfectionism

**Talk to yourself the way you would talk to a friend. A missed day is data, not a failure.**

**Why it works.** Many students fear that being kind to themselves will make them lazy. The research points the other way. Harsh self-criticism makes failure feel threatening, so you avoid looking at your mistakes. Self-compassion makes failure safe enough to examine and fix. In experiments, students who were encouraged to be self-compassionate after failing a test spent *more* time studying for a retest than those who were simply given a self-esteem boost.

**How to do it.**

1. When you slip, notice the inner voice. Would you say that sentence to a friend?
2. Rewrite it as you would for a friend: "That was a hard week. What got in the way, and what is one thing to change?"
3. Log the slip as data in your planner ("missed Tuesday: too tired after work") and adjust the plan, not your self-worth.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* After a bad quiz score a student writes "I didn't know the dates yet; I'll do 10 cards a day this week" instead of "I'm hopeless at history".
- *Non-trivial:* A self-taught programmer who skipped two weeks of a course is tempted to restart from lesson one "to do it properly". With a kinder lens he sees that the urge to restart is perfectionism. He reviews quickly, picks up where he left off, and treats the gap as a lesson about scheduling during busy work weeks.

### Compare less

**Students in competitive programmes (medicine, law, engineering, music conservatoires, top schools) often compare hours, grades and resources. Track your own progress instead.**

**Why it works.** We judge ourselves by comparison with others almost automatically; social psychologists have studied this since Leon Festinger's work in the 1950s. The problem is what we compare *with*. You see other people's highlight reel (the 12-hour library day, the top mark) and compare it with your own behind-the-scenes. Upward comparison with people who seem better often lowers mood and confidence, and hours studied is a poor measure anyway: one focused hour of self-testing can beat three hours of re-reading. Comparing yourself with your own past self is a fair race.

**How to do it.**

1. Keep a simple record of your own measures: practice scores, topics moved from "new" to "solid", cards reviewed.
2. Once a week, compare with last week or last month, not with classmates.
3. Mute or limit study-flex group chats and social media in exam season.
4. Use others for ideas ("what resource do you like for this?"), not for verdicts.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A school student stops asking friends "how many hours did you do?" and instead notes her own weekly quiz percentage.
- *Non-trivial:* A conservatoire violinist used to compare his practice hours with a classmate who practises six hours a day. Tracking his own recordings week by week showed his intonation improving steadily on three focused hours. He kept his schedule, and the comparison stopped costing him sleep.

### Sleep 7 to 9 hours

**Memory is consolidated during sleep. All-nighters lower exam performance.**

**Why it works.** During sleep the brain replays what you learned during the day and moves it from short-term storage into longer-lasting memory. Think of the day as writing notes on a whiteboard and sleep as copying them into a notebook before the board is wiped. Skip the copying and much of the day is lost. Sleep also restores attention and mood, which you need in the exam itself. A study of university students who wore activity trackers through a semester found that sleep duration, quality and consistency together explained nearly a quarter of the differences in their grades.

**How to do it.**

1. Aim for 7 to 9 hours (teenagers often need more), at roughly the same times every day, including weekends.
2. Protect sleep most in the week before exams, when it is most tempting to cut.
3. If you must choose, sleep and review in the morning rather than stay up late.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student who learned a set of new vocabulary in the evening remembers more of it the next morning than after the same number of hours awake.
- *Non-trivial:* A history student facing two exams on consecutive days plans her "late night" for two days *before*, not the night between. The night before the first exam she sleeps normally so that the material is consolidated and she is sharp, and does a short morning recall session instead of a late cram.

**Watch out.** Ongoing trouble falling or staying asleep is worth raising with a doctor (GP) or your student health service.

### Exercise

**Even a 20-minute walk improves mood, focus and sleep.**

**Why it works.** Movement increases blood flow to the brain and releases chemicals that support attention and the growth of new connections, especially in the hippocampus, a region central to memory. It also burns off some of the physical tension that stress builds up, a bit like letting steam out of a pressure cooker. You do not need a gym: regular moderate activity like brisk walking shows benefits.

**How to do it.**

1. Put movement in the plan like a class: a 20 to 30 minute walk, cycle, swim or session most days.
2. Use it as a break between study blocks, not as a reward you only earn at the end.
3. On exam days, a short walk before the exam can settle nerves.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student walks to the library the long way, 20 minutes, and arrives more alert than after the bus.
- *Non-trivial:* A self-taught coder who is stuck on a problem goes for a walk *with* the problem but without the screen. The movement lifts her mood, and the break gives her mind room to reorganise the problem, so she often comes back with the solution. Walking becomes both a wellbeing habit and a thinking tool.

### Food, caffeine and screens

**Regular meals, caffeine before early afternoon, and screens off before bed.**

**Why it works.** Your brain runs on a steady supply of energy, so long gaps without food make concentration and mood dip. Caffeine blocks the chemical signal of sleepiness, but it lingers for many hours: in a sleep-lab study, a strong dose taken six hours before bed still cut sleep by more than an hour, and people did not notice the loss. So the afternoon coffee quietly steals from tonight's memory consolidation. Screens at night keep the mind switched on and push bedtime later.

**How to do it.**

1. Eat regular meals on study days; keep a simple snack and water at the desk.
2. Set a caffeine cut-off around early afternoon (adjust to your bedtime: at least 6 hours before, more if you are sensitive).
3. Put your phone away, out of reach, 30 to 60 minutes before bed; read paper or listen to something calm.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student who used to have energy drinks at 6 pm switches to an earlier coffee and finds she falls asleep faster.
- *Non-trivial:* A nurse studying part-time on shifts cannot use a clock-based cut-off. She uses "no caffeine in the last 6 hours before my planned sleep", whatever time that is, and keeps a fixed wind-down routine after night shifts. The rule adapts to her life instead of breaking on it.

**Watch out.** Specific health conditions (diabetes, eating disorders, heart problems) need advice from a doctor, not general rules.

### Protected time off

**At least one half-day per week without study. Rest is part of the plan.**

**Why it works.** Recovery only happens when you actually switch off. Half-studying while feeling guilty is neither rest nor work. A protected block works like the rest days in an athlete's training plan: the gains come partly *during* the recovery, and athletes who skip it get injured. Planned time off also makes the study time more focused, because you know a break is coming and do not need to steal it.

**How to do it.**

1. Pick a fixed slot each week (for example Saturday afternoon) and write it in the planner as an appointment.
2. Fill it with something that is not a screen version of studying: friends, sport, a hobby, nature.
3. Keep it even in exam season, perhaps shorter. Cutting it to zero is usually what tips stress into burnout.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A school student keeps Sunday afternoon for football and does not open books, and finds Monday's session easier to start.
- *Non-trivial:* A PhD student with no external schedule finds herself working a little every day and never feeling rested. She sets a hard rule: no work from Saturday 1 pm until Sunday evening, with the shutdown ritual from "Define a stopping point" on Saturday morning. Her weekly output stays the same, but the quality of her weekday focus improves.

### Ask for help early

**University counselling, a doctor (GP), or student wellbeing services. Burnout and anxiety are common in demanding courses and are treatable.**

**Why it works.** Problems are smaller and easier to treat when caught early, the same way a small leak is easier to fix than a flood. Young people often delay asking because of embarrassment, worry about what others will think, or not knowing that what they feel counts as "bad enough". Knowing in advance where to go removes one of those barriers. You do not have to be in crisis to deserve support.

**How to do it.**

1. Find out now, while you are well, what your school, university or employer offers: counselling, wellbeing staff, disability or learning support, a tutor or mentor.
2. Book an appointment with a doctor (GP) if low mood, anxiety, sleep problems or exhaustion last more than two weeks or affect daily life.
3. Tell one trusted person (a friend, family member or tutor) how you are really doing.
4. If you are in immediate danger or think you might harm yourself, contact emergency services or a crisis line in your country straight away.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A first-year student who keeps waking at 4 am worrying about exams goes to the university counselling service and gets a few sessions plus practical tools.
- *Non-trivial:* A mature student with a job and children feels constantly behind and assumes it is just a time-management problem. Talking to a disability adviser reveals possible ADHD; an assessment and adjustments (extra exam time, recorded lectures) change the picture more than any planner could.

**Watch out.** The resources linked below are UK-based examples. In other countries, look for your institution's counselling service, your doctor, and national mental health organisations.

## Motivation and making study enjoyable

Motivation lasts when you feel in control of how you study, can see yourself getting better, and feel connected to others and to the reason you're doing it. Enjoyment usually follows competence: studying becomes more fun once it starts working.

That sentence summarises self-determination theory, one of the best-supported theories of motivation. It says people stay engaged without being pushed when three needs are met: **autonomy** (it feels like your choice), **competence** (you can see yourself getting better) and **relatedness** (you feel connected to people and to a purpose). Motivation is less like a fuel tank you either have or do not, and more like a fire: these three needs are the oxygen, wood and spark. The items below are practical ways to feed each of them, plus habit tricks that keep you studying on the days when motivation is low anyway.

> **📚 Further reading**
>
> - Article: [Theory](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/theory/), Center for Self-Determination Theory
> - Paper: Ryan, R. M. & Deci, E. L. (2000). [Self-determination theory and the facilitation of intrinsic motivation, social development, and well-being](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68). *American Psychologist*.

### Choose your own methods

**Experiment and keep what you like. Ownership of the method is itself motivating.**

**Why it works.** The same activity feels different when it is chosen rather than imposed: a walk you decided to take is pleasant, the same walk as a punishment is not. Feeling that the method is yours (autonomy) turns study from obedience into a project. A meta-analysis of choice experiments found that giving people a choice raised their intrinsic motivation, effort and performance, and that a few meaningful options worked better than very many.

**How to do it.**

1. Pick two or three well-supported methods from this compendium (for example flashcards, practice questions, blank-page recall).
2. Try each for a week on real material and note how it felt and how well you recalled things a few days later.
3. Keep the ones that worked for you and drop the rest without guilt.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student who hates making flashcards switches to answering past questions and finds he actually starts sessions on time.
- *Non-trivial:* A language learner is told to use a strict spaced-repetition app but finds it joyless. She keeps the spacing principle but owns the format: she records herself telling short stories with the week's words and re-tells them a few days later. Same science, her method, and she sticks with it.

**Watch out.** Choose *among* effective methods. "I like re-reading" is a preference, not a good reason, if your recall tests show it is not working.

> **📚 Further reading**
>
> - Paper: Patall, E. A., Cooper, H. & Robinson, J. C. (2008). [The effects of choice on intrinsic motivation and related outcomes: a meta-analysis of research findings](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18298272/). *Psychological Bulletin*.

### Make progress visible

**Question-bank percentages rising, a topic moving from "new" to "solid", a growing done list.**

**Why it works.** Learning is slow and invisible from the inside: you rarely *feel* yourself getting better day to day. Making progress visible feeds the need for competence, the way a progress bar makes a long download bearable. Studies of workers found that the single biggest booster of motivation on a given day was a sense of making progress, even small progress. A meta-analysis of goal experiments found that monitoring progress improves the chance of reaching goals, especially when progress is physically recorded.

**How to do it.**

1. Choose one or two honest measures (practice scores, topics marked "solid", problems solved without hints).
2. Record them somewhere you see daily: a wall chart, a spreadsheet, a topic list with colours.
3. Look back over weeks, not days, where the change is clearer.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A school student colours each maths topic red, amber or green, and watches the sheet turn green over the term.
- *Non-trivial:* A self-taught programmer keeps a log of "things I could not do last month that I can do now" (write a function from scratch, read an error message, use Git). On days when he feels stuck, the log shows that "stuck" is a feeling, not a fact.

**Watch out.** Measure what matters (recall, solved problems), not inputs like hours, which can rise while learning stalls.

> - Paper: Harkin, B. et al. (2016). [Does monitoring goal progress promote goal attainment? A meta-analysis of the experimental evidence](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/91437/). *Psychological Bulletin*.

### Gamify it

**Beat your own recall score, keep a forgiving streak (for example 5 of 7 days counts), earn points for sessions, race a friend through a question set.**

**Why it works.** Games are engaging because they give clear goals, immediate feedback and a sense of getting better, which is exactly what studying often lacks. Borrowing those features can help. The evidence is promising but mixed: a meta-analysis of gamification in education found small to medium positive effects on learning, motivation and behaviour, but the motivational effects were less stable in the strongest studies, and design matters. Points and badges alone tend to wear off; a story, a goal and some friendly collaboration last longer.

**How to do it.**

1. Compete with your past self first: today's score on 20 cards versus last week's.
2. Make streaks forgiving: "5 of 7 days" or a couple of allowed misses per month. A goal with a small built-in reserve keeps people going after a slip.
3. Add a social element if you like it: a team race through a question set, or a shared leaderboard among friends.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student gives herself a point per completed session and buys a nice coffee at 20 points.
- *Non-trivial:* A medical student and a law student studying together set up a weekly "boss fight": a mixed set of hard questions each has written for the other. Collaboration (writing good questions) and competition (answering them) are combined, and writing questions for someone else is itself strong retrieval practice.

**Watch out.** If keeping the streak becomes the goal, you will start doing easy reviews just to tick the box. The game should serve the learning, not replace it.

### Use fresh starts and forgive missed days

**When you fall off the plan, restart from the next natural landmark (a Monday, a new month, the start of a new topic) instead of waiting to "catch up" first.**

**Why it works.** After a bad week, the gap between where you are and where you "should" be can feel so large that starting again seems pointless. Researchers have found that people are more likely to start on goals at temporal landmarks such as the start of a week, month or term: the landmark draws a line between the "old me" who slipped and the "new me" who starts. You can create these landmarks on purpose. Habit research also suggests that missing a single day does not undo a habit; long gaps and inconsistency do.

**How to do it.**

1. When you slip, do not try to repay all the missed work. Pick the next landmark (tomorrow, Monday, the next chapter).
2. Restart with the normal plan, perhaps slightly smaller, and mark the restart in your planner.
3. If needed, schedule a short catch-up block later, separate from the daily routine.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student who missed four days of flashcards restarts on Monday with a capped review of 50 cards a day instead of facing a backlog of 800 at once.
- *Non-trivial:* An adult learning Spanish lost two months to a house move. Rather than restarting from lesson one, she declares the first day in the new flat her "fresh start", takes a quick placement quiz, and resumes from where the quiz puts her. The landmark gives her the motivation; the quiz keeps her from wasting it on material she still knows.

### Curiosity first

**Start a topic with a mystery: a puzzling case, a strange result, a "why does this happen?".**

**Why it works.** Curiosity arises when you notice a gap between what you know and what you want to know, an idea the economist George Loewenstein called the "information gap". The gap is mildly uncomfortable, like an unfinished sentence, and closing it is rewarding. Brain-imaging research found that when people were curious about a question, they remembered the answer better, and even remembered unrelated information shown while they were curious. Curiosity seems to put the brain into a "ready to learn" state.

**How to do it.**

1. Before reading a new chapter, find or write one puzzling question it answers.
2. Make a guess before you read. A guess gives you a personal stake in the answer.
3. Read to resolve the mystery, then check how your guess compares.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Before a lesson on the seasons, a student asks "why is it summer in Australia when it is winter in Europe, if the Earth is closer to the Sun in January?".
- *Non-trivial:* An economics student starts the topic of inflation with a real puzzle: why did prices in one country rise a hundredfold in a year while a neighbour's barely moved? He spends five minutes guessing explanations, then works through the theory as a detective checking which guesses survive. In medicine the same works with a puzzling patient case; in law, with a surprising court ruling.

### Connect it to your future use

**Ask "when would I actually need this?". For future patients, clients, a code base, a conversation in the language you are learning, a court case. Practical exposure (shadowing, placements, projects, real conversations) makes basic material feel relevant.**

**Why it works.** "Why do I need to know this?" is one of the biggest motivation killers. When you can see how a piece of knowledge will be used, it stops being an obstacle and becomes a tool. In a classroom experiment, high school science students who wrote short essays about how the topic related to their own lives became more interested and earned better grades, and the effect was largest for students who expected to do poorly.

**How to do it.**

1. At the end of a topic, write two or three sentences: "Where could I use this in my future work or life?"
2. Make it specific: a real situation, a real person, a real task.
3. Seek out small doses of real use: a placement, a project, a conversation with someone who uses this knowledge.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A language learner links this week's vocabulary on food to ordering at a market on her next trip, and practises the actual dialogue.
- *Non-trivial:* A computer science student bored by algorithm complexity looks at a slow app she uses daily and estimates why it gets slower as her data grows. The abstract O(n²) suddenly explains a real annoyance. A medical student does the same by linking a pharmacology table to a patient seen on placement; a law student, by tracing a dry statute through a real case.

**Watch out.** Not everything has an obvious use. Then it often helps to connect it to the next topic it supports ("I need this to understand X").

### Variety

**Rotate places (library, café, home), methods and formats to keep things fresh.**

**Why it works.** Monotony drains motivation: the tenth identical session in the same chair feels heavier than the first. Variety restores some novelty. There is also a memory bonus: in a classic experiment, people who studied the same material in two different rooms remembered it better than those who studied twice in the same room, probably because the memory became tied to more than one set of cues. That helps in an exam hall that looks nothing like your bedroom.

**How to do it.**

1. Have two or three study places and rotate them across the week.
2. Vary the format within a topic: questions one day, a diagram the next, explaining aloud the day after.
3. Keep the core (retrieval and spacing) constant; vary the packaging.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student does reading in the library, flashcards on the bus and practice questions at the kitchen table.
- *Non-trivial:* A music theory student rotates between written harmony exercises, analysing a piece she loves, and playing the progressions on the piano. The three formats are different angles on the same knowledge, so the variety is also deeper learning rather than just a change of scene.

**Watch out.** Variety should not become constant novelty-seeking. If you spend more time choosing where and how to study than studying, simplify.

### Study socially

**Study groups, quizzing friends, teaching younger students, online study rooms.**

**Why it works.** Other people feed the need for relatedness: you are part of something, and someone notices if you do not turn up. They also change how you learn. Simply *expecting* to teach material makes people organise it better and remember more of it, because they read like a teacher, looking for the structure and main points. Quizzing each other adds retrieval practice for free.

**How to do it.**

1. Find one to three people with similar goals and agree a regular time.
2. Start each meeting with everyone studying alone for a set block (quiet accountability), then quiz or explain to each other.
3. Rotate who "teaches" a topic each week.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Two friends meet on a video call twice a week, study silently for 50 minutes, then quiz each other for 10.
- *Non-trivial:* A history student volunteers to help a younger student with essays. Preparing to explain how to build an argument from sources forces him to make his own method explicit, and his own essays improve. Teaching, it turns out, is a study method in disguise.

**Watch out.** Groups can drift into chatting or into comparing (see "Compare less"). Keep a simple agenda.

### Small wins and rewards

**Celebrate finished blocks, not just finished exams.**

**Why it works.** An exam is weeks away; your brain cares far more about what happens in the next ten minutes. If the only reward is at the very end, today's session has no reward at all. Small, immediate rewards close that gap. One clever version is "temptation bundling": allowing yourself a treat *only* while doing the hard thing. In one study, people who could listen to gripping audiobooks only at the gym went to the gym more often.

**How to do it.**

1. Define a small unit of success: a finished block, a set of 20 questions, a section of an essay.
2. Attach a small, quick reward: a walk, a snack, a song, a tick on a chart.
3. Try bundling: a favourite podcast only while making flashcards, a nice café only for essay writing.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* After each 45-minute block, a student takes a 10-minute walk outside and calls it her reward.
- *Non-trivial:* A trainee accountant saves his favourite TV series for after his evening practice set, and only then. Over time, the practice set becomes the gateway to something he looks forward to, and starting gets easier.

**Watch out.** Keep rewards small and healthy. Big rewards for everything can make study feel like it is only worth doing for the prize.

### Identity, not willpower

**"I'm someone who reviews every day" lasts longer than relying on motivation each morning. Habits do the heavy lifting.**

**Why it works.** Willpower is a negotiation you have to win again every day. An identity settles the negotiation in advance: people act in line with who they think they are. Each session you do is a small "vote" for that identity, and the identity in turn makes the next session easier. In an experiment, asking people "how important is it to you to *be a voter*?" increased actual turnout more than asking about the importance of *voting*. Nouns that describe who you are seem to motivate more than verbs that describe what you do.

**How to do it.**

1. Write a short identity statement in the present tense: "I'm a person who practises every day", "I'm a programmer who writes code most days".
2. Make the daily action small enough that you can always cast your vote (5 minutes counts).
3. When you skip, ask "what would someone like that do now?", not "why am I so lazy?".

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* "I'm a reader" leads a student to read ten pages every night, even on busy days.
- *Non-trivial:* A nursing student who always thought of herself as "bad at maths" starts saying "I'm someone who checks drug calculations carefully". The new identity is about a behaviour she controls, not a talent she lacks, and it makes her practise calculations every week instead of avoiding them.

### Habit stacking

**Attach study to something you already do (coffee, then flashcards).**

**Why it works.** Existing habits are already wired to happen automatically. Attaching a new action to one of them lends it that automatic trigger, the way a new carriage is coupled to a train that already runs on time. Habits form by repeating a behaviour in the same context. In a study following people forming new daily habits, it took on average about 66 days (roughly two months) for a behaviour to feel automatic, with a wide range from 18 to 254 days; missing a single day did not derail the process. So expect weeks to months, not "21 days".

**How to do it.**

1. Use the formula: "After I \[current habit\], I will \[study action\]."
2. Choose a current habit that happens daily, at a fixed time and place.
3. Keep the study action small and specific at first (10 flashcards, one practice question).
4. Expect it to take around two months to feel automatic, and keep going on low-motivation days.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* "After I pour my morning coffee, I do my flashcards before opening any messages."
- *Non-trivial:* A shift worker without a regular morning uses a different anchor: "After I take my work shoes off, I do one practice question." The anchor travels with her shifts, so the habit survives an irregular schedule where a clock-based plan would fail.

### Rest without guilt

**Planned breaks and days off prevent the burnout that kills motivation.**

**Why it works.** Motivation is not only about starting; it is about still wanting to start in week ten. Students who never rest often find that motivation collapses all at once, not gradually. Planned rest protects the long game, like a marathon runner's steady pace. The key word is *planned*: a break you decided on in advance is recovery, while a break you slid into is guilt, and guilt is tiring in itself.

**How to do it.**

1. Put breaks into the plan: short breaks between blocks, a protected half-day each week (see "Protected time off" in the previous section).
2. When the break comes, take it fully. No "quick check" of notes.
3. If you need unplanned rest, label it as rest ("I'm taking tonight off") rather than drifting.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student works in 50-minute blocks with 10-minute breaks and stops at 6 pm, and finds she still wants to study on Friday.
- *Non-trivial:* A law student preparing for the bar exam notices her practice scores falling in week six despite more hours. She adds a full day off each week. Her scores recover within two weeks: the extra hours had been tired hours, and tired hours were not helping.

### Revisit your why

**Write down why you chose this subject and read it during hard weeks.**

**Why it works.** In hard weeks, attention narrows to the boring part: the next 200 cards, the dull chapter. Reconnecting with the bigger purpose widens the view again. Research found that students who had a purpose for learning that went beyond themselves (helping others, contributing to something) kept working longer on tedious tasks and resisted tempting distractions better. Purpose does not make boring tasks fun, but it makes them worth doing.

**How to do it.**

1. Write a short paragraph: why this subject, what you want to be able to do, who it will help (including your future self).
2. Keep it somewhere visible: the first page of your notebook, a pinned note on your phone.
3. Read it at the start of a hard week or when you catch yourself asking "what's the point?".

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student learning German writes "to talk with my grandmother in her language" and reads it before vocabulary sessions.
- *Non-trivial:* An engineering student's "why" is to build safe bridges in the region where she grew up. When statics problems feel endless, she does not just reread the note; she links the week's problem set to one real bridge failure she has read about. The purpose becomes concrete enough to carry her through a dull exercise. A medical student's why might be future patients; a teacher-in-training's, a future class.

### Make it beautiful, a bit

**A good desk, a nice notebook, music without lyrics or a focus playlist, as long as it doesn't become the task itself.**

**Why it works.** A pleasant place lowers the "cost" of sitting down, the way a tidy kitchen makes cooking more inviting. Small rituals (a lamp, a particular mug) also act as cues that tell your brain "now we study". Music needs care: songs with lyrics compete with reading for the same language-processing resources. In one experiment, reading comprehension scores dropped sharply with lyrical music, whether people liked the songs or not (about 38% correct versus about 61% in silence); instrumental music cost much less (about 55%).

**How to do it.**

1. Set up one study spot you like: good light, comfortable chair, what you need within reach, phone out of sight.
2. For reading and writing, silence is best; instrumental or ambient sound is a reasonable second choice if silence feels oppressive. Save lyrics for routine tasks like organising notes.
3. Cap the setup time: five minutes, then start.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student buys a notebook she enjoys writing in and finds it easier to start her blank-page recall each day.
- *Non-trivial:* A design student spends a whole Sunday perfecting a digital planner and colour-coded templates, then realises he has studied nothing. He sets a rule: aesthetic tweaks only *after* the day's study, as a reward. The beauty stays, but it stops being procrastination in disguise.

**Watch out.** If decorating notes, choosing playlists or organising apps takes longer than the studying, it has become the task itself.

## Exams and exam season

If the term went well, exam season is mostly about practice under exam conditions rather than learning new material. The ratio shifts towards questions and past papers.

A useful picture: during the term you build the knowledge; in exam season you practise getting it out, fast, in the exact shape the exam asks for. Memory works best when the way you practise matches the way you will be tested (researchers call this transfer-appropriate processing). Someone who has only read about swimming is not ready for a race, however many books they read. This applies to a school final, a university exam, a bar exam, a driving theory test, a language certificate or a coding interview.

### Start practice questions early

Start from the first weeks of the term, not the last two.

**Why it works.** Answering questions is not only a way to measure learning; it is one of the strongest ways to produce it (the testing effect). Students who test themselves remember far more a week later than students who reread the same material, even though rereading feels more productive at the time. Starting early also spreads the questions out, so you get the spacing benefit for free, and you discover your misunderstandings while there is still time to fix them.

**How to do it.**

1. After each week's lessons, do a small set of questions (5 to 15) on that week plus a few older topics.
2. Read the explanation for every question you got wrong or guessed.
3. Do not save the question bank or past papers "for later so they stay fresh". A fresh bank you never use is worth nothing.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* a school student does the end-of-chapter questions in the geography textbook on the day the chapter is finished, not in the revision week.
- *Non-trivial:* a law student uses past exam problem questions from week three, even though she can only answer a third of each one. Writing a partial answer tells her which rules she cannot yet apply, which no amount of reading would show.

**⏱ Time cost.** One exam-style question with its explanation is \~2-3 min (\~4-6×). Still one of the best uses of study time.

>
> - Article: [What is retrieval practice?](https://www.retrievalpractice.org/why-it-works), Pooja Agarwal, RetrievalPractice.org
> - Paper: Roediger & Karpicke (2006). [Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x). *Psychological Science*.
> - Paper: Yang et al. (2021). [Testing (quizzing) boosts classroom learning: A systematic and meta-analytic review](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000309). *Psychological Bulletin*.

### Past papers and question banks

These are the best predictor of exam content. Notice what kinds of questions keep coming up.

**Why it works.** Examiners are people with habits. The same course is usually examined by the same people, with the same favourite topics, question shapes and command words ("compare", "evaluate", "derive"). Past papers show you the target you are actually aiming at, not the whole syllabus in equal weight.

**How to do it.**

1. Collect several years of past papers (from the course page, the department, the exam board or older students).
2. Tally the topics and question types across years. A simple table is enough.
3. Do at least one recent paper early to see the level, and keep the most recent ones for timed mocks near the end.
4. Use question banks for volume and past papers for realism.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* a student tallying five years of chemistry papers sees that a titration calculation appears every year, and makes sure she can do one in her sleep.
- *Non-trivial:* a history student notices that the essay questions never ask "describe" but always "how far" or "to what extent". He stops memorising narratives and starts practising arguments with a judgement in the first paragraph.

**Watch out.** Past papers show patterns, not promises. Learn the whole course, but weight your time by what the patterns show.

> - Paper: Rowland (2014). [The effect of testing versus restudy on retention: A meta-analytic review of the testing effect](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559). *Psychological Bulletin*.

### Simulate the exam

Use the same time limit, no notes, and the same time of day if possible. Do at least one full mock.

**Why it works.** An exam tests more than knowledge: it tests pacing, stamina, handling pressure and recalling without any cues. Each of those can be practised. A full mock shows whether you run out of time, where your concentration dips (often in the third hour), and which question types make you freeze. It also makes the real exam feel familiar, which lowers anxiety.

**How to do it.**

1. Pick a past paper you have not seen.
2. Set a timer for the real duration, sit at a desk, phone away, no notes.
3. Mark it honestly using the mark scheme, then fill in your error log.
4. Check your timing per question: where did you lose minutes?

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* a student taking a language listening exam does a full past listening paper with the audio played once only, as in the real exam, not with pauses.
- *Non-trivial:* a candidate for a 4-hour professional accounting exam does a full mock on a Saturday morning at 9 am and discovers that his accuracy drops sharply after hour three. He changes his order of attack (long questions first while fresh) and practises a 2-minute reset routine between sections.

**⏱ Time cost.** The length of the exam plus about the same again for marking and analysis. Worth it 1 to 3 times per major exam.

### Error log

For every wrong answer, note the topic, why you got it wrong (did not know it, misread the question, confused it with something else, ran out of time) and the fix.

**Why it works.** A wrong answer is the most valuable thing a practice session produces, but only if you learn from it. Errors that you notice and correct, especially ones you made with high confidence, tend to be remembered very well afterwards (Metcalfe 2017). Logging the *type* of error also reveals patterns that a score alone hides: you might not have a knowledge problem at all, but a reading-the-question problem.

**How to do it.** Keep a simple table:

| Question / topic | What I answered | Correct answer | Error type | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stats: p-value interpretation | "Probability H0 is true" | Probability of data this extreme if H0 true | Misconception | Card + explain to a friend |
| Physics Q7 | Units in cm | Should be m | Careless | Always write units in the first line |

Review the log weekly, and redo the logged questions a few days later without looking at the answer.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* a maths student sees that 6 of her last 10 errors were sign mistakes, not concept errors, and adds a 30-second check of signs before moving on.
- *Non-trivial:* a medical student's log shows she keeps confusing two similar conditions. Instead of more single facts, she makes a comparison table of the two and practises mixed questions on both. The pair stops appearing in her log.

**⏱ Time cost.** About 1 to 2 minutes per error (\~2-4×). Cheap for what it gives.

### Weak topics first

Use question-bank statistics or your error log to choose what to review.

**Why it works.** It is pleasant to revise what you already know, because it feels fluent and reassuring. But a point gained on a weak topic counts exactly the same as a point on a strong one, and is much easier to win. Students left to themselves tend to reread rather than test, and so they misjudge what they know (Karpicke, Butler & Roediger 2009). Data from questions corrects that illusion.

**How to do it.**

1. Sort your topics by score (question bank stats, mock results or error log counts).
2. Spend most revision time on the bottom third, but keep light reviews of strong topics so they do not fade.
3. Re-test each weak topic after working on it to check it actually moved.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* a driving theory candidate scores 95% on road signs and 60% on stopping distances, so she spends her evening on stopping distances.
- *Non-trivial:* an economics student finds his weakest topic is also the least examined. He gives it a modest share of time and spends more on the second-weakest topic, which appears every year. Weakness times weight, not weakness alone, decides the order.

### Interleave in the final weeks

Mix courses and topics, the way the exam will.

**Why it works.** In a chapter's exercises, you always know which method to use: it is the one from the chapter. In an exam, the hardest part is often recognising which kind of problem you are looking at. Mixing problem types forces you to practise exactly that choice. In one classic study, students who practised mixed maths problems did far better on a later test than students who practised in blocks, even though blocked practice felt easier at the time (Rohrer & Taylor 2007).

**How to do it.**

1. Build question sets that mix topics from across the course in random order.
2. Before solving each one, name the topic or method ("this is a conservation of energy problem").
3. Expect it to feel slower and harder; that is the point.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* a language learner mixes past, present and future tense exercises in one set, instead of a page of each.
- *Non-trivial:* a programmer preparing for coding interviews shuffles problems from arrays, graphs, dynamic programming and strings, and gives himself two minutes to decide the approach before writing code. His weak point turns out to be spotting when a problem is a graph problem in disguise, something blocked practice never revealed.

**⏱ Time cost.** \~1× extra: it changes the order, not the amount, though it feels \~20-30% slower.

### Know the format

Every exam format needs its own practice: multiple-choice questions (MCQs), short answers, essays, problem sets, oral exams, practical exams and speaking tests.

**Why it works.** Knowing something and producing it in a particular format are different skills. Recognising the right option among five is easier than writing it from nothing; writing it is different from saying it aloud to an examiner; saying it is different from doing it with your hands. Memory is most accessible when practice and test use the same kind of processing (Morris, Bransford & Franks 1977), so practise in the format you will face.

**How to do it.** Find out the format, the marking scheme and the timing, then practise that exact thing:

- **MCQs:** lots of timed questions; practise eliminating wrong options.
- **Short answers:** answer from a blank page in the word limit; compare with the mark scheme's key points.
- **Essays:** practise planning a thesis and structure in 5 to 10 minutes, and write some full timed essays.
- **Problem sets (maths, physics, economics):** solve unseen problems with full working; marks often come from the method.
- **Oral exams and vivas:** explain topics aloud to a partner who interrupts with questions.
- **Practical exams:** rehearse the actual actions with a checklist, aloud, with a partner timing you. In medicine these are OSCEs (timed clinical stations); in science they may be lab practicals; in music, auditions.
- **Coding interviews:** solve problems while talking through your reasoning, on a whiteboard or a plain editor without autocomplete.
- **Language speaking exams:** record yourself answering typical prompts for 2 minutes, then listen back.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* a student with an open-book exam practises finding things fast in her book (tabs, an index page), because looking things up eats the time.
- *Non-trivial:* a philosophy student who writes excellent essays at home freezes in his first oral exam. For the next one he practises with a friend who asks "but why?" after each sentence, and learns to give short answers with one example instead of long monologues.

### No new resources in the last week

Stick to what you know.

**Why it works.** A new book or video series in the last week feels like progress but mostly brings new terminology, new framing and new gaps you will not have time to close. It also eats time that should go to retrieval and mocks. Worse, it can unsettle what you know ("this book says it differently, which is right?"). In the final week, consolidation beats expansion.

**How to do it.**

1. Before the final week, decide your final set of materials: your notes, your cards, your error log, and past papers.
2. Allow one exception: a targeted look-up to fix a specific error.
3. Spend the week on recall, mixed questions and one more mock.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* a student who discovers a popular revision video series five days before the exam adds it to a list "for next year" and keeps going with her own cards.
- *Non-trivial:* a certification candidate realises a whole topic is missing from his notes. Instead of starting a new 10-hour course, he reads the official exam guide section on it once, makes 10 cards and does 20 questions. Accepting a partly covered topic is better than sacrificing everything else.

### Protect sleep before the exam

Sleep matters more than one more late-night session.

**Why it works.** During sleep, the brain replays and stabilises what you learned during the day, so sleep is part of studying, not a break from it (Diekelmann & Born 2010). Sleep loss also hurts exactly what exams need: attention, working memory and reasoning. A study of university chemistry students found that sleep over the weeks before a test was linked to their grades, while sleep on the single night before was not (Okano et al. 2019). The lesson is double: one good night will not rescue a month of bad sleep, and one all-nighter throws away memories you built.

**How to do it.**

1. In exam season, keep regular sleep and wake times, also at weekends.
2. Stop studying at least an hour before bed; the last-minute extra is worth less than the sleep it costs.
3. Caffeine early in the day only.
4. If you cannot sleep the night before, rest anyway and do not panic: one poor night has less effect than people fear.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* a school student stops revising at 21:30 the night before her biology exam and does a 10-minute card review in the morning instead.
- *Non-trivial:* a student with exams on consecutive days resists the urge to study until 2 am for the second one. He does a short recall session after the first exam, sleeps normally, and uses the morning for a quick mixed review. His second exam goes better than the first, the opposite of last year.

### Calm exam-day nerves

Some nervousness helps; too much blocks memory. A few simple routines keep it in the helpful range.

**Why it works.** Anxiety uses up working memory: part of your mind is busy worrying, so less is left for the question. Two short interventions have good evidence. Writing about your worries for about 10 minutes before an exam improved test scores for anxious students, apparently by offloading the worry onto paper (Ramirez & Beilock 2011). Telling yourself that a racing heart is your body preparing to perform, rather than a sign of danger, improved maths scores in students preparing for a graduate admissions test (Jamieson and colleagues). And the best long-term cure is regular low-stakes testing all term: in one large classroom study most students said quizzes made them less anxious about exams (Agarwal et al. 2014).

**How to do it.**

1. All term: frequent, low-stakes self-testing, so tests become routine.
2. Before the exam: 5 to 10 minutes of writing down your worries, then put the paper away.
3. At the desk: a few slow breaths with a long exhale; say "this is energy, not danger".
4. If you blank on a question: move on, and let your mind work on it in the background.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* a student who always panics in maths tests writes "I'm worried I'll forget the formulas and fail" in the corridor, then feels calmer going in.
- *Non-trivial:* a language learner dreads the speaking part of her exam. She practises with recorded mock interviews weekly for a month. By exam day the format is boring rather than frightening, and her nerves are about the content, not the situation.

### During the exam

Read each question carefully and know what it is asking before you answer, move on from hard questions and come back at the end.

**Why it works.** In an exam, time is the scarcest resource, and it is easy to sink ten minutes into one hard question while three easy ones go unanswered. Marks are usually equal per question type, so collect the easy ones first. Coming back later also helps: a fresh look, or a cue from another question, often unlocks what was stuck. And contrary to the popular advice "never change your first answer", studies show that changed answers more often go from wrong to right; people remember the painful wrong changes more vividly (Kruger, Wirtz & Miller 2005).

**How to do it.** Start with a quick look at the whole paper and divide your time by the marks. Then, by format:

- **MCQs:** for long case-based questions, read the last line (the actual question) first, then the stem with that in mind. Eliminate wrong options. Flag and move on, then come back.
- **Essays:** spend 5 to 10 minutes planning: thesis, 3 or 4 points, evidence for each. A planned essay that is shorter beats an unplanned one that wanders.
- **Maths and science problems:** do the problems you can do first. Show your working, because method marks count even when the final answer is wrong.
- **Orals and speaking exams:** pause before answering; it is fine to say "let me think for a moment". Structure your answer: point, example, conclusion.
- **Practicals:** follow your rehearsed checklist and say what you are doing.
- **All formats:** leave 5 to 10 minutes to check, and change an answer when you have a clear reason, not just a vague doubt.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* a student in a 2-hour physics exam skips a nasty question 3, finishes 4 to 8, then returns to 3 with 25 minutes left and solves part of it.
- *Non-trivial:* a history student plans her essay for 8 minutes while classmates start writing at once. Her plan reveals that her best evidence fits a different argument than her first idea, and she changes her thesis before writing a word, instead of halfway through.

### After the exam

Note what surprised you and adjust your method for the next block.

**Why it works.** Most students look at the grade and move on, which wastes the most honest feedback they will ever get on their study method. A short reflection while the exam is fresh shows whether your preparation matched what was asked. Many universities use "exam wrappers", short reflection sheets after each exam, for exactly this reason: students who reflect on how they prepared tend to change their strategy, not just their effort.

**How to do it.** Within a day or two, write answers to:

1. What surprised me (topics, format, difficulty, timing)?
2. Where did I lose marks: knowledge, misreading, time, nerves?
3. Which preparation helped most, and which was wasted?
4. What one thing will I change for the next exam?

When results or feedback arrive, add the details to your error log.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* "Surprised by how many graph questions there were. Next time: practise graphs from week 1."
- *Non-trivial:* a nursing student realises she knew the content but lost marks because her short answers missed the key terms in the marking scheme. For the next module she practises writing answers against mark schemes, and her score rises without any extra study hours.

## Myths and low-yield habits to drop

These feel productive but give little lasting learning for the time spent. Keep them as short warm-ups at most.

| Habit or belief | Why it falls short | What to do instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Re-reading notes many times | Builds familiarity, not recall | Read once, then test yourself |
| Highlighting large parts of text | Passive; everything looks important | Highlight little, then turn highlights into questions |
| Learning styles ("I'm a visual learner") | Matching teaching to a preferred style has not been shown to improve learning | Use whatever format fits the content: diagrams for anatomy or circuits, maps for history and geography, words for arguments, sound for languages and music |
| Long marathon sessions | Attention and memory drop after long unbroken blocks | Shorter, spaced blocks with breaks |
| Rewriting notes neatly | Takes hours and is mostly copying | Summarise from memory instead |
| Watching videos passively | Feels like learning, but little sticks | Pause and recall after each section |
| Multitasking with phone and messages | Each switch costs focus and time | Phone in another room during focus blocks |
| All-nighters before exams | Sleep loss hurts memory and reasoning | Spread the work; sleep before exams |
| More resources means more learning | Spreads attention thin | One spine, targeted extras |
| Hours studied as the measure of progress | Hours don't equal learning | Measure recall, question scores and topics mastered |

Almost every habit in this table shares one trap: it produces a *feeling* of learning (fluency, effort, busyness, coverage) without the thing that actually builds memory, which is pulling information back out of your head. The fix in each case is not to study more, but to swap the habit for something that makes you retrieve, apply or check. None of these habits is useless in small doses; the problem is when they become the main event.

### Re-reading notes many times

Re-reading builds familiarity, not recall. Read once, then test yourself.

**Why it feels productive.** The second and third time through, the text reads smoothly. That ease (psychologists call it *processing fluency*) feels exactly like knowing. But it is recognition: the page is in front of you, so of course it looks familiar. The exam will not show you the page. It is like believing you know the way to a friend's house because you recognise it from the passenger seat.

**What the evidence says.** In the large review by Dunlosky and colleagues (2013), re-reading was rated *low utility*: it helps a little, mostly right after reading, and much less than self-testing. A survey by Karpicke, Butler and Roediger (2009) found that re-reading was by far the most common strategy students reported, while very few used self-testing as their main one. The most popular habit is one of the least effective.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* You read a page about the causes of the French Revolution three times and feel ready. Close the book and list the causes: you get two of five.
- *Non-trivial:* A programmer re-reads the documentation for a function until it "makes sense", then cannot write the call without looking. Writing the call from memory once, then checking, would have shown the gap in 30 seconds and fixed it.

**What to do instead.** Read once carefully, close the source, and answer two or three questions about it from memory. Re-read only the parts you got wrong.

**⏱ Time cost.** Re-reading a paragraph takes \~1 min (\~2×). Two recall questions take about the same time (\~2×) and do far more for retention.

### Highlighting large parts of text

Highlighting is passive, and when half the page is yellow, everything looks important. Highlight little, then turn highlights into questions.

**Why it feels productive.** Highlighting gives your hands something to do and leaves visible evidence of work: a page full of colour looks like a page you have studied. But the decision "this matters" is not the same as "I can recall this". Heavy highlighting also postpones the hard choice of what is truly important.

**What the evidence says.** Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated highlighting and underlining *low utility*. In most studies it did not beat simply reading, and in some it slightly hurt performance on tests that required inference, perhaps because it focuses attention on isolated facts rather than on how they connect.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A biology student highlights 70% of a chapter. Next week, the highlights tell her nothing about what to review because almost everything is marked.
- *Non-trivial:* A law student allows herself one highlight per paragraph, only for the rule or the holding. After reading, she turns each highlight into a margin question ("What must be shown for negligence?") and later covers the text and answers them. The highlight becomes the start of retrieval rather than the end of studying.

**What to do instead.** Read a section first, then mark at most one key phrase per paragraph. Convert each mark into a question or a flashcard, and test yourself on those.

**⏱ Time cost.** Highlighting a paragraph takes \~1-1.5 min (\~2-3×). Writing one recall question in the margin and answering it later costs about the same and actually builds memory.

### Learning styles ("I'm a visual learner")

Matching teaching to a preferred style has not been shown to improve learning. Use whatever format fits the content.

**Why it feels productive.** People really do have preferences: some enjoy diagrams, some prefer listening. It feels respectful and intuitive to match the format to the person. The catch is that a preference is not the same as a benefit. You may *like* podcasts more than reading and still learn the material equally well, or better, from a diagram.

**What the evidence says.** Pashler and colleagues (2008) set out what a study would need to show to support learning styles: people with style A learn better with method A, *and* people with style B learn better with method B. Very few studies used that design, and the ones that did mostly failed to find the effect. What does help is matching the format to the *content*: maps for geography, diagrams for circuits and anatomy, spoken practice for pronunciation. And combining words with pictures (dual coding) helps nearly everyone.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A "visual learner" tries to learn Spanish pronunciation from colourful charts. Listening and repeating aloud would work better for anyone, because pronunciation is sound.
- *Non-trivial:* An economics student who calls himself "verbal" avoids graphs and memorises paragraphs about supply and demand. Sketching the curves and shifting them for each scenario would make the logic visible in a way words cannot.

**What to do instead.** Ask "what format does this material naturally take?" rather than "what kind of learner am I?". Then, whatever the format, test yourself on it.

### Long marathon sessions

Attention and memory drop after long unbroken blocks. Use shorter, spaced blocks with breaks.

**Why it feels productive.** Six hours in the library feels heroic, and it looks good when you tell people. But the last hours of a marathon are usually the least productive: you read the same line three times, and you are tired enough that what you take in is shallow. It also crowds all the practice into one day instead of spreading it out, which wastes the spacing effect.

**What the evidence says.** On long, monotonous tasks, performance tends to drift down over time (the *vigilance decrement*). Ariga and Lleras (2011) found that brief, rare switches away from a long task prevented that decline. Separately, decades of spacing research (Cepeda et al., 2006) show that the same total study time, split across several sessions, gives much better long-term memory than one big block.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Instead of one 6-hour Sunday session on chemistry, do three 50-minute blocks with 10-minute breaks, and a 30-minute review on Wednesday.
- *Non-trivial:* A music student practising a hard passage for two hours straight starts repeating mistakes as fatigue sets in. Three 20-minute focused sessions across the day, each starting with the passage from memory, lead to cleaner playing by evening.

**What to do instead.** Work in blocks of 25 to 50 minutes with short breaks that actually rest your attention (walk, water, look out of the window, not your phone). Spread a topic across days rather than finishing it in one sitting.

### Rewriting notes neatly

Rewriting takes hours and is mostly copying. Summarise from memory instead.

**Why it feels productive.** Clean, colour-coded notes are satisfying to make and to look at, and the hours feel well spent. But copying with the source open needs almost no recall: your eyes go from one page to the other, and the information passes through without being pulled out of memory.

**What the evidence says.** Generating information yourself is remembered better than copying it, and retrieval is one of the strongest ways to generate. Karpicke and Blunt (2011) found that students who practised free recall of a text learned more than students who spent the same time building detailed concept maps with the text in front of them. Even an elaborate, "active" note-making method lost to simply writing down what you remember.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Instead of rewriting a history lecture neatly, close your notes and write the main events and causes from memory on a blank page. Then fill gaps in another colour.
- *Non-trivial:* A physics student's "final notes" project would take a weekend. Instead she writes one-page summaries *from memory* for each chapter, checks them against the textbook, and corrects them in red. The red marks show exactly where to focus, and the pages still become tidy revision sheets.

**What to do instead.** Write from memory first, then check and correct. Keep notes good enough to test yourself from; tidiness is optional.

**⏱ Time cost.** A neat rewrite of one lecture often takes 1-2 hours. Blank-page recall of the same lecture plus filling gaps takes about 20-30 minutes (around \~4× per paragraph), and it shows you what you actually know.

> - Paper: Karpicke, J. D. & Blunt, J. R. (2011). [Retrieval practice produces more learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199327). *Science*.

### Watching videos passively

Videos feel like learning, but little sticks. Pause and recall after each section.

**Why it feels productive.** A good explainer makes everything look obvious while you watch. The teacher does the thinking, and their fluency becomes your feeling of understanding. It is like watching someone ride a bicycle: easy to follow, no help when you get on.

**What the evidence says.** Szpunar, Khan and Schacter (2013) split an online lecture into segments. Students who got a short test after each segment mind-wandered about half as much, took more notes and remembered more at the end than students who re-studied the same material between segments. The test, not the extra exposure, made the difference.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Watching a 12-minute video on photosynthesis, pause every 3-4 minutes and say aloud the two main points so far.
- *Non-trivial:* A programming student following a tutorial pauses before each step and tries to write the next lines of code herself, then plays the video to compare. It takes longer, but she can build the project alone afterwards, which pure watching rarely achieves.

**What to do instead.** Watch with a question in mind. Pause at each section break, recall the key points without looking, then continue. After the video, close it and do a short blank-page recall.

**⏱ Time cost.** One recall pause per section adds about 30 seconds (\~1×). Over a 15-minute video, that is 2-3 extra minutes for a large gain.

### Multitasking with phone and messages

Each switch costs focus and time. Put the phone in another room during focus blocks.

**Why it feels productive.** Answering messages while studying feels efficient: two things done at once. In reality the brain switches back and forth, and each switch has a cost, a few seconds of re-orienting and a thread of thought lost. A 50-minute block with ten "quick checks" holds much less real focus than its length suggests.

**What the evidence says.** Ophir, Nass and Wagner (2009) found that people who habitually juggle many media streams were *worse* at ignoring irrelevant information and slower at switching between tasks than light multitaskers. Later studies found smaller and less consistent differences, but the basic finding that switching between tasks costs time and accuracy is well established in attention research.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Reading a novel for literature class with the phone face-up, you check it after every notification and have to re-read paragraphs. With the phone in the next room, the chapter takes 20 minutes instead of 40.
- *Non-trivial:* A maths student solving a proof keeps a chat open "only for urgent things". Each glance breaks the chain of steps she is holding in working memory, so she restarts the reasoning repeatedly. Batching messages into the break after each block saves the chain.

**What to do instead.** Phone out of sight (another room is best), notifications off, and a set time to check messages between blocks. Keep a sheet of paper for "I must remember to..." thoughts instead of acting on them.

### All-nighters before exams

Sleep loss hurts memory and reasoning. Spread the work and sleep before exams.

**Why it feels productive.** The night before an exam, more hours of studying seem obviously better than sleep. Panic makes the trade feel urgent. But the night you skip is the night your brain would have used to stabilise what you studied, and you walk into the exam slower, less able to reason and with worse recall.

**What the evidence says.** Sleep plays an active role in consolidating memories: during sleep, newly learned material is replayed and stabilised (Diekelmann & Born, 2010). Sleep loss also reduces attention and working memory, which you need to read questions carefully and solve problems. In a large university course, Okano and colleagues (2019) found that students who slept longer, better and more regularly over the weeks before exams scored higher. Interestingly, the single night before a test mattered less than the sleep pattern over the preceding weeks, so the best protection is regular sleep through the term, not only on the last night.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* Instead of reviewing vocabulary until 3 a.m., do a final 30-minute recall session in the evening and sleep 8 hours. The words you reviewed are still there in the morning.
- *Non-trivial:* An engineering student with two exams in two days plans backwards: the hardest problem sets go three and four days before, the last evening is only light review of the error log, and bedtime is fixed. She trades two late hours of tired reading for a clear head on problems that need reasoning, not just recall.

**What to do instead.** Plan so that the last day is review, not new material. Protect 7 to 9 hours of sleep before exams, especially those that need reasoning or problem solving.

### More resources means more learning

Extra resources spread attention thin. Pick one spine and add targeted extras.

**Why it feels productive.** Buying a new book, bookmarking a channel or downloading another deck gives an instant feeling of progress and safety ("now I have everything"). Each source also explains things slightly differently, which feels like deeper coverage. But every hour spent taking in a new source is an hour not spent retrieving from the one you already have.

**What the evidence says.** There is little direct research on "number of resources", but the logic follows from retrieval research: testing yourself on material beats studying it again (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). A second or third source is mostly more input. It helps only when it fixes a specific gap.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* A student learning German has three textbooks, two apps and a podcast, and finishes none. Picking one course book as the spine and one app for vocabulary gets her further in a month.
- *Non-trivial:* A medical student uses lecture slides as the spine and a question bank for retrieval. When one topic (acid-base balance) still makes no sense, she watches one targeted video on it, then goes straight back to questions. The extra source has a job and an end.

**What to do instead.** See "Managing many sources": one spine per course, and two questions before opening anything new: will this be tested, and does it fix a specific confusion?

### Hours studied as the measure of progress

Hours don't equal learning. Measure recall, question scores and topics mastered.

**Why it feels productive.** Hours are easy to count and easy to compare with friends. They also reward the feeling of effort, whatever the effort was spent on. But two hours of re-reading and two hours of practice questions are not the same two hours.

**What the evidence says.** Plant and colleagues (2005) found that, on its own, the number of hours students spent studying did not predict their grades; study time only started to matter once the quality of the study conditions was taken into account. In research on expertise, Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (1993) argued that what builds skill is *deliberate practice*: focused work at the edge of your ability, with clear goals and feedback. Later research suggests practice explains only part of the differences between people, but the core point holds: the kind of hours matters more than the count.

**Examples.**

- *Simple:* "Studied 3 hours" says little. "Answered 40 questions on cell biology, 70% correct, up from 55% last week" says a lot.
- *Non-trivial:* A pianist who logs 4 hours a day mostly plays pieces she already knows. Her teacher has her spend 45 minutes on the four bars she gets wrong, slowly, recording herself. Her practice log shows fewer hours, and her playing improves faster.

**What to do instead.** Track outputs, not inputs: recall ratings, question-bank scores per topic, topics moved from "new" to "solid", and gaps found in blank-page recall. Use hours only to check that you are showing up regularly.

- Kang, S. H. K. (2016). [Spaced repetition promotes efficient and effective learning: Policy implications for instruction](https://doi.org/10.1177/2372732215624708). *Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences*. A short, readable overview of spacing for teachers and students.
- Gollwitzer, P. M. & Sheeran, P. (2006). [Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1>). *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*. "If X, then I will Y" plans had a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals across many studies.
- Karpicke, J. D., Butler, A. C. & Roediger, H. L. (2009). [Metacognitive strategies in student learning: Do students practise retrieval when they study on their own?](https://doi.org/10.1080/09658210802647009). *Memory*. Most students re-read; few test themselves.

- Diekelmann, S. & Born, J. (2010). [The memory function of sleep](https://doi.org/10.1038/nrn2762). *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*.
- Ophir, E., Nass, C. & Wagner, A. D. (2009). [Cognitive control in media multitaskers](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0903620106). *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*.
- Ericsson, K. A., Krampe, R. T. & Tesch-Römer, C. (1993). [The role of deliberate practice in the acquisition of expert performance](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.100.3.363). *Psychological Review*.
